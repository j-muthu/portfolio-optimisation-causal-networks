\begin{abstract}
\noindent Hierarchical portfolio allocators typically use 
the return correlation matrix as an input to their allocation. 
However, this matrix lacks any notion of directionality or causality.
Causal discovery algorithms ameliorate this, producing
a directed graph (DAG) which can be decomposed into a skeleton
(which assets are linked) and edge orientations
(which assets causally affect others).
This thesis addresses the following question: when the
correlation matrix in hierarchical portfolio allocation is
replaced by a causal graph, is there a performance
improvement, and how much of any improvement is attributable
to the graph's skeleton versus its edge orientations?

I answer this question by providing an identical DAG to
two classes of allocators: one that only takes in the graph's
skeleton, and one that takes in the whole graph, including its
edge orientations. This decomposes the performance gain into
the two aspects of the DAG. I then compare these allocators
to HRP as a baseline. I estimate this decomposition over 
2007--2024, using 4 estimation windows 
(9 months to 2 years), on a 99-asset subset of the S\&P~500. 
I use DYNOTEARS and VARLiNGAM as causal discovery algorithms,
with ridge-Granger as a robustness check.

I find that replacing the correlation matrix with a causal DAG
yields a positive but statistically insignificant increase 
in the Sharpe ratio. Around the 1-year estimation window,
the graph's skeleton accounts for most of the performance gain,
while the edge orientations contribute most at the extremes
(9 month and 2 year windows). However, the edge orientations'
contribution is due to the DAG's removal of the common
market factor, as providing a de-factored covariance matrix
as an allocator input reproduces the performance gain from the
edge orientations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Background and motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Portfolio optimisation allocates capital to different
assets in order to maximise returns for a given level of 
risk. Since Markowitz's seminal mean-variance model
\cite{markowitz1952portfolio}, the covariance
(or equivalently, correlation) matrix of returns has
been the main statistical object used for optimisation.
Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP) is a recent
method \cite{lopezdeprado2016hrp} that extends the 
mean-variance literature and aims to address
the matrix instability problems that affected earlier 
models. HRP first clusters assets based on their 
correlational distance, producing a dendrogram (binary
tree), then recursively bisects down that tree and 
allocates capital to clusters inversely proportional
to their estimated variances.

However, correlation is an undirected metric: 
it records which assets co-move, but not which assets
move others \cite{lopezdeprado2023causal}. Crucially,
it lacks causal structure, so is prone to spurious
correlation \cite{simon1954spurious}.
Causal discovery algorithms provide a directed alternative
to the correlation matrix. Score-based structure learning algorithms
(such as NOTEARS \cite{zheng2018notears} and its temporal extension
DYNOTEARS \cite{pamfil2020dynotears}) can fit directed acyclic graphs
(DAGs) that can represent which assets are linked and the direction
of influence between them. Some papers have applied these DAGs to
equity markets \cite{howard2025causal,tang2024trading}, 
but do not use them for weighting or allocation within 
hierarchical portfolio allocators.

However, current hierarchical methods like HRP are not
able to represent directed objects like DAGs,
because HRP's clustering methodology requires symmetric matrices.
But a DAG can be separated into two information sources:
its skeleton, an undirected structure that displays which asset
pairs are causally linked; and its edge orientations, which indicate
the direction of each causal link. The skeleton can be directly
provided as an input to hierarchical allocators by symmetrising
the adjacency matrix representation of the graph. The edge
orientations cannot, as symmetrisation discards directionality,
so incorporating their information requires novel allocators.

\section{The research question}
\label{sec:question}

The thesis addresses a central research question:

\begin{quote}
When the
correlation matrix in hierarchical portfolio allocation is
replaced by a causal graph, is there a performance
improvement, and how much of any improvement is attributable
to the graph's skeleton versus its edge orientations?
\end{quote}

\noindent I briefly define the terms in the question here, and
expand on them in more detail later on in the report.
First, hierarchical portfolio allocation specifically refers to
HRP, where capital is invested fully and long-only. I invest over
a fixed 99-asset subset of the S\&P~500, with the portfolio
rebalanced monthly between 2007 and 2024. The asset ``universe''
is discussed in further detail in Chapter~\ref{chap:discovery},
the allocation protocol in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.
Second, the correlation matrix is the matrix originally used in HRP,
the lookback window's sample correlation
\cite{lopezdeprado2016hrp}. This feeds into the baseline HRP
control (see Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity}).
Third, a discovered causal graph is the output of one of the causal
discovery algorithms (DYNOTEARS, VARLiNGAM, with ridge-regularised
Granger as a robustness check). It is a contemporaneous (no lags)
asset-to-asset graph that's re-fitted at each rebalance
at 4 different estimation windows 
(189, 252, 378 and 504 trading days; roughly 9 months to 2 years).
See Chapter~\ref{chap:discovery} for more details.
Fourth, performance improvement is defined as the out-of-sample 
annualised Sharpe ratio of daily portfolio returns, net of 
investment cost. I also use several other metrics that account 
for multiplicity (see Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}), including the 
Deflated Sharpe ratio, Hansen's SPA, and the Model Confidence Set
\cite{bailey2014deflated,hansen2005spa,hansen2011mcs}.
Finally, I compare the relative performance gain attributable to
the graph's skeleton versus its edge orientations using a 
``fixed-graph ablation'', where an identical graph $M$ feeds
a variety of allocators that differ only in the information they
extract from the graph. The skeleton-only allocator, \skelsamp{},
symmetrises $M$ before clustering, while three 
orientation-aware allocators consume the directed matrix itself
(either through the allocation step, the ordering step, or both).
See Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity} for further details.

The research question rests on two premises, both of which I test:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
    \item There must be a gain to decompose: the causal graph must
    improve on the correlation matrix for portfolio allocation. I test
    this in Section~\ref{sec:r1} by feeding HRP the symmetrised causal
    graph (the allocator denoted \skelsamp{}) and comparing it with
    standard sample-correlation HRP. The decomposition remains
    informative even if this premise fails, since it compares graph-fed
    allocators against each other on identical inputs.
    \item Some allocator must be able to consume orientation, which
    symmetric clustering cannot. Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity}
    constructs the orientation-aware family and verifies that the
    skeleton-only allocators and the HRP baseline are orientation-blind.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent With both premises in place, I am then able
to isolate the performance gain of the two components of the graph.
The skeleton's contribution over the HRP baseline measures the
value of knowing which assets are causally linked to each other. 
The edge orientations' contribution over the skeleton measures
the additional value of knowing the direction of the causal links
between assets, holding the graph fixed. The two marginal
contributions sum to the total performance gain of the causal
graph over the correlation-based allocator. 

In addition, after isolating the contributions of the two 
aspects of the graph, further questions arise regarding what drives the
performance gain within those two aspects. Specifically:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Does the skeleton's performance gain arise purely from the 
    actual discovered causal graph structure, or can the performance gain
    be reproduced by any sparse dependence structure of a similar density,
    regardless of whether it has been created by causal discovery?
    \item Does the performance gain from edge orientations
    arise specifically from the directional content of the causal DAG's
    edges, or does it arise from another aspect of the DAG, such as the
    implied covariance, which can be reproduced without the need for
    a causal graph?
\end{itemize}
\noindent I answer these questions in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

\section{Challenges and contributions}
\label{sec:challenges}

There are three key technical challenges I must address in this report.
Together, they bring together three research domains,
which have previously not been applied jointly to portfolio allocation:
causal structure learning; hierarchical portfolio construction with
directed, asymmetric inputs; and rigorous backtesting. The three
challenges are as follows:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item Correct attribution of performance gains to different aspects of 
  the causal graph (Chapter~\ref{chap:discovery}). Naively replacing the
  correlation matrix with a causal graph means that different aspects
  of the graph (e.g. its skeleton, its edge orientations, and its
  implied covariance) all inform allocation jointly. This means
  the contributions of specific aspects cannot be individually isolated.
  No prior work has performed this isolation; it requires a credible,
  reproducible graph. 
  \item Getting hierarchical portfolio allocators to accept 
  asymmetric DAGs as
  an input (Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity}). Hierarchical allocators
  use symmetric distance matrices as inputs. Simply symmetrising
  a DAG's adjacency matrix would discard the edge orientation information, but this project's research question needs to isolate orientation's effect on performance. There is currently no established way to feed
  an asymmetric DAG adjacency matrix into the clustering or allocation step of these allocators.
  \item Performing statistical inference at realistic effect sizes
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}). The main metric for the ``performance improvement'' in the project's research question is the
    differences in net Sharpe between allocators. These differences are
    likely to be small relative to standard error, given the 18 years of
    monthly rebalances and the heavy-tailed nature of returns. Also, this
    study evaluates \rsNtrials\ configurations (across different discovery methods, allocators, and window sizes). Deciding whether any
    difference in net Sharpe is not just an artefact
    of the search over many configurations requires statistical inference
    that accounts for this search and identifying the statistical power of the tests I use. 
    I also need to identify whether the performance gains I attribute to
    the skeleton and the edge orientations of the graph are actually products
    of the causal graph, and aren't just byproducts from other, non-graph-specific mechanisms that the graph just happens to possess
    (such as generic shrinkage or market-factor removal).
\end{itemize}

\noindent The three challenges above are respectively addressed by the three
novel contributions below:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item A fixed-graph decomposition design, plus a reproducible
    discovery pipeline (attribution; Chapter~\ref{chap:discovery}). 
    The same per-window DYNOTEARS or VARLiNGAM graph feeds several different
    allocators that incrementally take in more information from the DAG,
    from none (correlational \HRP{}), to skeleton-only, to orientation-aware.
    This means that each increment isolates one aspect of the graph:
    the skeleton's contribution is the skeleton-only allocator minus \HRP{}, and the
    orientations' contribution is the orientation-aware allocator minus the
    skeleton-only one. 
    I ensure that the allocators receive the identical graph and are replicable.
  \item Creating a new family of orientation-aware allocators
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity}). This is the core methodological novelty
    of my research. I provide HRP with two directed inputs, one per step of the
    algorithm. At the allocation step, the sample covariance is replaced by
    the covariance implied by the DAG's structural equation model.
    At the bisection step, HRP's clustering dendrogram is replaced by
    the DAG's topological order. The skeleton-only allocator uses the
    implied covariance (\skelsem{}), then there are two allocators that use
    the DAG's topological order: \orientsamp{} uses it alone, while
    \orientsem{} uses both the topological order and the implied covariance.
  \item Conducting a pre-registered, controlled evaluation with multiple metrics (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}). Each comparison is tested using
  a stationary block bootstrap \cite{politis1994stationary},
    which resamples the daily return series in blocks so the
    resulting $p$-values account for autocorrelation and heavy tails.
    To account for all \rsNtrials\ configurations evaluated, I use the Deflated Sharpe
    ratio, which corrects the best-performing configuration based on the number of trials examined. I also use Hansen's SPA test and the Model Confidence Set
    to identify whether any member of a set of strategies outperforms the benchmark once the whole set is tested jointly, including a pooled test over both strategies and
    estimation windows. 
    I also compute the minimum detectable effect at $80\%$ power
    (about \rsContrastMDEOrient{} Sharpe for the edge orientation and
    \rsContrastMDETotal{} for the total). This means that any null results
    act as upper bounds on the effect, rather than as evidence of no effect at all.
    I also test whether the causal graph is the real driver of any performance gain over the correlational allocator: given that the DAG provides a different covariance as an input to HRP, that difference in covariance, rather than the informational value of the edge orientations, may be the driver.
    To test this, I create three other allocators that make a similar change to covariance without providing a graph. The first replaces the sample
    covariance with a Ledoit--Wolf shrunk covariance to identify whether the performance gain was purely an artefact of shrinkage. The second removes the common market factor from the returns before
    computing the covariance to identify whether that market-factor removal alone was the driver of performance gains.
    The third just clusters on a partial-correlation
    network that has the same density as the skeleton, which indicates whether the performance gain was achievable by any sparse network (rather than needing a causal network specifically).
    I also test on 2025--2026 out-of-sample data, and check that there is still a positive performance gain for both the skeleton-only allocator over the HRP baseline and the orientation-aware allocators over the skeleton-only allocator, matching the in-sample positive values. 
    As a robustness check, I also rerun the whole experimental design using a second hierarchical allocator, HERC. This verifies whether the conclusions are specifically dependent on HRP as a choice of hierarchical allocator.
\end{itemize}
\section{Quantified benefits}
\label{sec:benefits}

All of the numbers below come from the protocol described in
Section~\ref{sec:question}: net of 5\,bps transaction costs, monthly
rebalances, 2007--2024. Significance comes from the Politis--Romano
stationary block bootstrap \cite{politis1994stationary}. I report the
numbers as they came out, including where the effect is small or zero.
No published method sets hierarchical portfolio weights from a discovered
causal graph (Howard et al.\ \cite{howard2025causal} build causal graphs
on the S\&P~500 but never allocate from them), so there is no external
number to compare against. The external benchmark is therefore the
\HRP{} baseline, L\'opez de Prado's published HRP construction, run on the
identical protocol. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} gives the full detail.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item The causal graph improves on the correlation matrix at every
    estimation window. \skelsem{} beats \HRP{} by $+0.018$ to $+0.032$ net
    Sharpe across the 4 windows, which is worth $+0.4$ to $+0.7$ percentage
    points of net CAGR per year. 19 of the 20 DYNOTEARS graph-versus-\HRP{}
    comparisons are positive (36 of 40 across both discovery methods). At
    the 378-day window, \orientsem{} has the highest Deflated Sharpe ratio
    of all \rsNtrials\ configurations I evaluated ($\rsDtwosDSRWthree$). On
    the 2025--26 out-of-sample data, every sign I specified in advance came
    back consistent with the in-sample decomposition.
  \item The edge orientations contribute at every window ($+0.009$ to
    $+0.023$), and most at the 189-day and 504-day extremes. At the
    504-day window they repair the skeleton-only allocator's collapse
    (\rsDoneSharpeWfive{} for \skelsem{} against \rsDzeroSharpeWfive{} for
    \skelsamp{}). This gain is unchanged when costs rise from 0 to 20\,bps,
    and \skelsem{} trades less than \skelsamp{}. Family-wise, Hansen's SPA
    over the narrowed family gives $p=\rsSpaDirWfive$ at 504 days, and
    nothing rejects once the 4 windows are pooled into one test.
  \item The skeleton contributes $+0.022$ at the 252-day window. There it
    also beats a partial-correlation skeleton of matched density, built
    without any causal discovery, by $+0.025$ ($p=0.008$). So at that
    window the discovered structure matters, and sparsity alone does not
    reproduce the gain. This answers the first sub-question of
    Section~\ref{sec:question}.
  \item The mechanism behind the orientation gain is market-factor
    removal. A de-factored covariance built without any graph reproduces
    the orientation gain at every window (the residual is between $-0.011$
    and $+0.004$), whereas Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage does not (\skelsem{}
    beats it by $+0.018$ to $+0.042$). So the gain from the SEM-implied
    covariance comes from its assumption of independent shocks, which
    removes the common market factor, and not from the direction of the
    edges. This answers the second sub-question of
    Section~\ref{sec:question}. The observed effects are about half the
    minimum detectable effect, so the result is a bounded null: the edge
    orientations are worth roughly $0$ to $0.04$ Sharpe. The effect also
    depends on the discovery method. It is absent under VARLiNGAM graphs
    at all 4 windows.
  \item The graph route beats the driver-and-sensitivity route (HSP) that
    it replaces. Plain \HRP{} (\rsCorrSharpe) already beats HSP as
    published ($0.371$) at 252 days on the identical protocol. Every
    graph-route allocator is seed-free, runs in about 40 seconds per
    backtest, and sits above the maximum of HSP's \rsSeedN-seed
    distribution (Appendix~\ref{app:hsp}).
\end{itemize}

In summary, the causal graph's value to hierarchical allocation is stable
across estimation windows, but where that value comes from shifts with the
window. Family-wise significance appears only under the post-hoc narrowed
family. The contribution is the allocator family and the fixed-graph
design that made this measurable. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} restates
the full answer with its caveats.

\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:background}
% ============================================================

This project sits between two literatures: portfolio construction and
causal discovery. Rather than survey both, I analyse in depth the two
works that the project builds on directly, one from each. The first is
L\'opez de Prado's Hierarchical Risk Parity and his later work on backtest
rigour (Section~\ref{sec:hrp-theme}). HRP supplies the allocator whose
clustering step Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity} modifies, and the backtest
work supplies the inferential standard I adopt in
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}. The second is Howard, Lohre and Mudde's
work on causal networks over the S\&P~500
(Section~\ref{sec:howard-theme}). It is the closest causal-finance work
to this thesis, and it stops one step short of the question I ask.
Section~\ref{sec:cd-tools} then summarises the facts from the
causal-discovery literature that the pipeline relies on, and
Section~\ref{sec:gap} states the research gap.

\section{Theme I: hierarchical allocation and honest backtests
(L\'opez de Prado)}
\label{sec:hrp-theme}

Mean--variance optimisation \cite{markowitz1952portfolio} requires the
inverse of a covariance matrix. For a 100-asset universe estimated on a
1-year window, that matrix is noisy and near-singular, so the optimiser
amplifies the estimation error and produces concentrated, unstable
weights. Hierarchical Risk Parity \cite{lopezdeprado2016hrp} avoids the
inversion with a three-step scheme. First, clustering: convert the
correlation matrix $\rho$ into the distance
$d_{ij} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\rho_{ij})}$ and build a single-linkage
dendrogram. Second, quasi-diagonalisation: reorder the assets by the
dendrogram's leaf order, so that large covariances sit near the diagonal.
Third, recursive bisection: split the ordered list in half, allocate
between the two halves in inverse proportion to their cluster variances,
and recurse into each half. No matrix is inverted, so HRP is robust to
ill-conditioning. In the paper's Monte Carlo experiments it beats both
mean--variance and inverse-variance portfolios out of sample
\cite{lopezdeprado2016hrp}.

Two observations about this scheme drive the thesis. First, the
dendrogram exists only to produce an ordering. The allocation step
(recursive bisection) takes an ordered list and cluster variances, and it
never reads the tree itself. This means that any procedure which produces
an economically meaningful ordering of the assets can replace the
clustering step entirely. This observation motivates the causal-ordered
bisection of Section~\ref{sec:d2}. Second, every input to the clustering
and quasi-diagonalisation steps is symmetric by necessity. Linkage
clustering merges clusters by comparing distances, and a distance is
symmetric by definition. So if the true data-generating process contains
one-way influence (asset $A$ moves asset $B$, and $B$ does not move $A$
back), no correlation-fed hierarchical allocator can represent that fact.
This is the problem raised in Section~\ref{sec:motivation}. It is not a
flaw in how correlation is estimated. It is a limit on what the
clustering step can accept. Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity} addresses how
to get directional information into HRP without breaking what makes it
work.

The limitation applies to the whole family of hierarchical allocators,
not just to HRP. HRP has spawned several variants: hierarchical
clustering-based asset allocation and its equal-risk-contribution version
\cite{raffinot2017hierarchical}, and nested clustered optimisation
\cite{lopezdeprado2020mlam}. These vary the linkage rule, the risk
measure or the within-cluster optimiser, but every one of them clusters
on a symmetric similarity matrix at the first step. A decomposition of
what directed structure is worth at that step therefore applies to the
family, not to one algorithm. I use HRP because it is the simplest and
most studied member, so any effect I find is attributable to the input
rather than to the allocator's elaborations. Whether the measured
decomposition also transfers to a second member of the family is an
empirical question. I test it on a HERC variant \cite{raffinot2018herc}
in Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}, with a mixed result.

One member of the family changes the input rather than the way the tree
is read. Hierarchical Sensitivity Parity (HSP) \cite{rodriguez2023hsp}
keeps HRP's clustering and recursive bisection, but clusters assets on
the similarity of their sensitivities to a set of common drivers rather
than on return correlation. For each asset, a feed-forward neural network
maps $K$ macro-financial drivers (rates, commodities, currencies,
volatility indices) to the asset's return. The gradient of the fitted
network with respect to the drivers, obtained by automatic
differentiation, is the asset's sensitivity vector $s_i$. The distance
$\lVert s_i - s_j \rVert_2$ then replaces the correlation distance. The
motivation is the commonality principle
\cite{rodriguez2023hsp,rodriguez2025causal}. If the drivers are common
causes of the assets, in the sense of Reichenbach's common-cause
principle \cite{hitchcock2020reichenbach}, then distance in sensitivity
space measures shared exposure to those causes, and clustering on it is
argued to diversify systematic and idiosyncratic risk at the same time.
HSP is therefore the published hierarchical allocator closest to this
thesis, because it clusters on something other than correlation.
However, it is not a causal method. The drivers are selected by their
cumulative correlation with the assets, the sensitivities are gradients
of a conditional expectation rather than structural effects, and the
distance it gives to HRP is symmetric, so the limitation above applies
to it unchanged. Also, because the sensitivities come from a trained
network, the allocation inherits a source of randomness (the network
initialisation) that correlation-based HRP does not have.

A separate and well-established way to improve allocation is to improve
the covariance input rather than the allocator. Linear shrinkage pulls
the noisy sample covariance towards a structured target with an
analytically optimal intensity, trading a small bias for a large
reduction in estimation variance \cite{ledoitwolf2004}. Removing a common
market factor through a single-factor model, and keeping the residual
(de-factored) covariance, suppresses the dominant source of co-movement
noise in equity panels. Both methods condition the covariance estimate
without asserting any graph over the assets.
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} uses exactly these two estimators,
Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage and a de-factored residual covariance, as
graph-free controls for the mechanism behind the SEM-implied covariance.

The same author's later work supplies the evaluation standard. The core
claim of the backtest-overfitting literature
\cite{bailey2014deflated,lopezdeprado2023causal} is that the hard problem
in quantitative finance is not finding a configuration that looks good in
a backtest. It is establishing that the configuration's performance is
not an artefact of the search that found it. Two instruments follow. The
Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio \cite{bailey2012psr} replaces the Gaussian
sampling theory of the Sharpe ratio, which is untenable at the excess
kurtosis of ${\approx}\,\rsKurtosis$ I measure here, with a version that
corrects for the sample's skewness, kurtosis and length. The Deflated
Sharpe Ratio \cite{bailey2014deflated} then raises the benchmark from
zero to the best Sharpe expected from $N$ independent trials under the
null of no skill, which directly discounts selection bias. For comparing
a family of strategies against a benchmark, White's Reality Check
\cite{white2000reality} and Hansen's SPA test \cite{hansen2005spa}
bootstrap the null that no candidate beats the benchmark, and the Model
Confidence Set \cite{hansen2011mcs} returns the subset of strategies that
are statistically indistinguishable from the best. I apply this battery
to every claim in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}. The Deflated Sharpe
Ratio deflates each headline Sharpe against all \rsNtrials\
configurations I evaluated, while the SPA and MCS run over the smaller
family of candidates relevant to each question. Several effects that look
like headlines do not survive it, and I report that as part of the
result.

\section{Theme II: causal graphs on equities, without allocation
(Howard, Lohre and Mudde)}
\label{sec:howard-theme}

Howard, Lohre and Mudde \cite{howard2025causal} is the closest prior
work. It is a large-scale application of DYNOTEARS to S\&P~500
constituents over 1989--2022 (about 357 names per estimated network),
re-estimated on a rolling basis. Structurally, this is the same discovery
machinery as Chapter~\ref{chap:discovery}. Three of their empirical
findings shape my design. First, lagged edges are negligible at
daily-to-monthly horizons: the contemporaneous block $W$ contains
essentially all the structure. This justifies my use of the
contemporaneous asset-to-asset block as ``the graph'', and the truncation
to one lag in Section~\ref{sec:asset-graph}. Second, causal networks
carry regime information that correlation misses. Rising network density
precedes lower returns and flags recessions, and their centrality-based
signal earns its alpha in stable rather than stressed periods. This
motivates the regime-conditional evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:regimes},
where I find that the orientation effect follows their calm-regime
pattern. Third, their overall verdict is measured: causal networks
complement, rather than consistently beat, correlation-based methods. My
research question sharpens that claim into a decomposition, by asking
whether the complement operates through the skeleton or through the edge
orientations.

The gap this project fills is defined by what they do not do. Their three
use-cases (peer groups for factor neutralisation, a low-centrality
factor, and a density-based market-timing signal) all use the graph as a
similarity structure or as a scalar diagnostic. The graph is never handed
to an allocator. No portfolio weights are ever computed from the
discovered network, and the direction of the edges is used only
implicitly (through centrality). So their pipeline cannot ask how much of
the graph's allocation value lies in its orientations. Tang
\cite{tang2024trading} builds trading signals from time-series causal
discovery (including VARLiNGAM) and finds that computational cost is the
binding constraint. I address this warning with a content-keyed discovery
cache (Section~\ref{sec:cache}) rather than by shrinking the experiment.

\section{The causal-discovery toolkit}
\label{sec:cd-tools}

The pipeline needs three facts from the causal-discovery literature
\cite{spirtes2000causation,kaddour2022causalml}.

First, score-based discovery scales and accepts priors. Constraint-based
methods (PC, FCI) scale poorly with dimension and rely on faithfulness.
That assumption is fragile in markets, where offsetting mechanisms can
leave causally connected pairs with near-zero marginal correlation
\cite{howard2025causal}. NOTEARS \cite{zheng2018notears} recasts
acyclicity as the smooth constraint
$h(W)=\operatorname{tr}(e^{W\circ W})-d=0$, which turns DAG learning into
a continuous optimisation. DYNOTEARS \cite{pamfil2020dynotears} extends
it to the structural VAR $X = XW + \sum_p Y_p A_p + Z$, with acyclicity
enforced on the contemporaneous $W$ only. It natively supports
forbidden-edge lists, which is how I enforce the directional prior
described in Chapter~\ref{chap:discovery}. It is the primary method here.

Second, non-Gaussianity identifies direction. The LiNGAM family
\cite{shimizu2006lingam} exploits non-Gaussian residuals to identify a
unique causal ordering. VARLiNGAM \cite{hyvarinen2010varlingam} is its
structural-VAR form and serves as my robustness comparator. Its
identification rests on heavy-tailed residuals. Financial returns satisfy
this at short windows, but lose it progressively at longer ones, because
aggregation pulls residuals towards Gaussianity. The results repeatedly
show this mechanism.

Third, a directed graph without trustworthy magnitudes exists in closed
form. A ridge-regularised VAR(1) coefficient matrix is a Granger-causal
\cite{granger1969investigating} directed graph. It gives a defensible
directional ordering, but its shrunken one-lag coefficients are not
structural effect sizes. This combination makes it a natural control for
separating the two things that ``orientation'' might contain: the arrows
themselves, and the magnitudes fitted to propagate shocks.

\section{Research gap}
\label{sec:gap}

Table~\ref{tab:gap} positions the work. The \HRP{} baseline of
Section~\ref{sec:question} is the HRP row. Reading across the columns:
HRP fixes the allocator but is blind to direction at the clustering
step; HSP changes what HRP clusters on, but selects its drivers by
correlation and still gives the allocator a symmetric distance; Howard et
al.\ discover directed graphs at scale but never allocate from them. None
of the three sets hierarchical portfolio weights from a discovered
asset-to-asset causal graph. So none of them can decompose the graph's
allocation value into skeleton and edge orientations, which is what the
research question demands and
Chapters~\ref{chap:sensitivity}--\ref{chap:evaluation} deliver.

I need to be careful with the novelty claim, because allocating from a
graph over assets is not itself new. The correlation-network tradition
builds portfolios from minimum-spanning-tree and PMFG filtrations of the
correlation matrix. Its most direct result is that weight placed in the
peripheries of the network outperforms \cite{pozzi2013spread}, and there
is also work on network-centrality portfolio selection
\cite{peralta2016network}. Clustering assets on a graph and then
allocating down its structure descends from that tradition, and the
skeleton-only allocator in this thesis is its conceptual descendant.
Directed-graph allocation is also not unprecedented. Granger-causality
networks over financial institutions are an established systemic-risk
measurement tool \cite{billio2012econometric}, and V\'yrost, Ly\'ocsa and
Baum\"ohl set portfolio weights directly from such directed networks
\cite{vyrost2019network}. What remains, to my knowledge, is a narrower
gap. No prior work sets hierarchical portfolio weights from a discovered
structural, score-based causal graph (as opposed to a network of pairwise
predictive tests), and none decomposes the allocation value of such a
graph into its skeleton and its edge orientations. That decomposition is
what this thesis delivers.

\chapter{Discovering the Graph}
\label{chap:discovery}
% ============================================================

This chapter addresses the first challenge of
Section~\ref{sec:challenges}: attributing performance gains to specific
aspects of the graph. The graph enters allocation through several
channels (its skeleton, its edge orientations and its implied
covariance). To isolate them, I need a graph that is credible, is held
fixed and bit-reproducible across every allocator, and is cheap enough to
refit 215 times per configuration. An earlier route in this project,
which selected drivers using neural-network sensitivities, was
seed-dependent and could not meet that standard. The output of this
chapter is a reproducible rolling discovery pipeline with four parts: a
directional prior, a content-keyed cache, a determinism fix, and a single
extraction function through which every allocator receives its graph.
Together they produce the object at the centre of the research question:
a credible, reproducible asset-to-asset causal graph at every monthly
rebalance of the 2007--2024 backtest.

Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data.
Section~\ref{sec:discovery-run} describes the rolling discovery, the
directional prior, and my empirical check that the prior matters.
Section~\ref{sec:asset-graph} describes the extraction function and what
the resulting graphs look like. Section~\ref{sec:comparators} describes
the two comparator graphs, and Section~\ref{sec:cache} the engineering
that makes the whole programme tractable and reproducible.

\section{Data layer}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Universe.} The target scale is the S\&P~100. It is large
enough to be representative and small enough that discovery at every
rebalance remains sweepable, since fit cost grows steeply with dimension
(Section~\ref{sec:cache}). Official historical S\&P~100 membership is
paywalled, and it cannot be reconstructed from S\&P~500 records because
the index is chosen with undisclosed sector-balance discretion. I
therefore use the standard academic substitute: the top names by CRSP
market capitalisation among historical S\&P~500 members at each
rebalance. The union of these across rebalances gives a fixed 99-asset
universe over 2007--2024. Appendix~\ref{app:universe} lists the tickers
and the drivers. Prices and point-in-time shares outstanding come from
WRDS/CRSP, which includes names that later delisted. This is essential
for a realistic 2008--09, when several dozen large-cap financials
disappeared. A thin Yahoo Finance fallback covers the most recent days.
One aspect of the delisting treatment needs stating. Names that delist
mid-sample leave the panel at their last quoted price, and I do not apply
CRSP delisting returns, so the final-day loss of an involuntary delisting
is not captured. Every allocator shares this treatment. It shifts
absolute performance levels slightly upwards, but it does not affect the
pairwise contrasts, which difference two strategies on the same panel.
Names that list after 2007 (post-2007 listings and merger successors) are
kept through a per-date eligibility mask rather than by truncating every
other series' history. The top-by-market-cap construction remains an
approximation of the official index, and I note it as a limitation in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\paragraph{Driver pool.} Discovery runs on the joint panel of the 99
assets and 33 plausibly exogenous macro-financial series from FRED and
Yahoo Finance: Treasury rates and slopes, credit spreads, commodities, FX
pairs, volatility indices, and international-equity ETFs. Because the
drivers are in the joint fit, the asset-to-asset block is estimated
conditional on the common macro environment. This absorbs co-movement
that the drivers explain, which would otherwise appear as asset-to-asset
edges. I exclude series that are not exogenous to a US large-cap
portfolio (sector ETFs, US index returns and factor portfolios).

\paragraph{Stationarity and transforms.} SVAR-based discovery assumes
weakly stationary inputs. I transform each series according to its type:
log-returns for price-like series; first differences for yields and
spreads, which cross zero, so logs are undefined; year-on-year changes
for monthly macro levels, which also removes seasonality; and no
transform for the volatility indices, which are already stationary. A
uniform ``log everything'' rule would break on the rates block. I fix the
transforms a priori by series type, rather than choosing them by test in
each window, so that each variable means the same thing in every rolling
window. This is a prerequisite for comparing graphs across time. I then
check stationarity with both the augmented Dickey--Fuller (ADF) and
Kwiatkowski--Phillips--Schmidt--Shin (KPSS) tests. The two are
complementary because their nulls are opposite, so agreement is a
stronger signal than either alone. Failures are flagged and logged; I do
not use them to drop series.

\section{Rolling discovery and the directional prior}
\label{sec:discovery-run}

I chose the primary discovery method against three requirements. It must
scale to the ${\sim}132$-variable joint panel at every rebalance of a
sweepable grid. It must accept the directional prior natively, rather
than by deleting edges after the fit. And it must return a DAG, because
the allocators of Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity} rely on the exact
total-effect algebra that a DAG's nilpotent adjacency provides. DYNOTEARS
meets all three. The comparators of Section~\ref{sec:comparators} each
relax one requirement, so they double as probes of whether that
requirement mattered.

At each rebalance date $t$, I fit DYNOTEARS \cite{pamfil2020dynotears} on
the $z$-scored joint panel $X=[\,D \mid A\,]$ over the lookback window
(189 to 504 trading days, the 4-window grid of
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}) with lag $p=1$. Lagged structure beyond one
step is negligible at these horizons (Section~\ref{sec:howard-theme}).
The fit yields the contemporaneous matrix $W$, with acyclicity enforced,
and the lagged matrix $A_1$, both over the joint variable set. Throughout
the report, $M[i,j]$ is the effect of variable $i$ on variable $j$.
Appendix~\ref{app:tables} tabulates the hyperparameters.

\paragraph{Enforcing the prior.} The directional prior is that drivers
may cause assets, but individual large-cap stocks do not move the 10-year
Treasury yield at these scales. DYNOTEARS accepts a forbidden-edge list
(\texttt{tabu\_edges}). I populate it with every asset$\to$driver pair at
every lag, which forces those entries of $W$ and $A_1$ to zero. VARLiNGAM
enforces the same prior through its prior-knowledge mechanism, plus a
projection after the fit.

\paragraph{Verification of the prior.} A prior should only be kept if it
has a measurable effect. I refit DYNOTEARS with and without the prior on
6 windows spanning distinct regimes (2008, 2011, 2014, 2018Q4, 2020 and
2022). Without the prior, the optimiser spends 35--48\% of total edge
mass on economically implausible asset$\to$driver edges. Admitting those
edges distorts the legitimate driver$\to$asset block by 52--72\% in
$L_1$. It also changes which drivers carry the most edge mass in a
meaningful minority of windows: the Jaccard overlap of the top drivers
with and without the prior is $0.62$--$1.00$, and is lowest in the 2008
and 2022 stress regimes (Figure~\ref{fig:prior}). So the prior is not
cosmetic. It removes a large block of spurious reverse-causation mass at
negligible cost, and because the asset-to-asset block is estimated
jointly with the rest, it protects the graph that the allocators use.

\section{Extracting the asset graph}
\label{sec:asset-graph}

The fixed-graph ablation is only valid if every allocator sees the
identical graph. I therefore centralise extraction in one function
rather than leaving it to each allocator. At each rebalance, the function
reads the asset-to-asset block $M$ of the fitted contemporaneous matrix.
It then restricts rows and columns together to the assets eligible at
that date, so that late-listing names can never put the graph out of
step with the returns panel. It applies the magnitude threshold $\tau$
(default $0$; swept in the sparsity analysis), and verifies acyclicity (a
sub-block of a DAG is a DAG). It also computes the per-asset structural
residual variances on the fit window, which the SEM-implied covariance of
Section~\ref{sec:d-family} needs. It uses the window's stored
$z$-statistics for this, so the residuals correspond exactly to the data
the graph was fitted on.

\paragraph{What the graphs look like.} The DAGs are far from degenerate,
and stably so across estimation windows. At every window length from 189
to 504 days, the DYNOTEARS asset graphs average ${\sim}614$--$673$
directed edges (a density of $6.3$--$6.9\%$ of possible ordered pairs).
Their mean longest directed path is ${\approx}50$ nodes out of 99, which
indicates deep chains of influence rather than a shallow hub-and-spoke
structure. So the graphs contain substantial orientation structure for
the orientation-aware allocators to use. Whatever the evaluation finds,
it will not be because the graphs are trivially symmetric. One diagnostic
is missing: I did not quantify month-to-month edge persistence
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The same diagnostic flags a comparator
limitation. VARLiNGAM's unpruned $B_0$ saturates to $32\%$ density at the
189-day window, where there are $n=189$ observations against $d=132$
variables, whereas DYNOTEARS' $\ell_1$ penalty holds density steady.

\paragraph{What ``credible'' can mean without ground truth.} One
objection must be addressed directly. No true causal graph of the market
is available, so the discovered graphs cannot be scored against an answer
key. I therefore validate the pipeline on everything that can be checked.
The directional prior has a quantified effect (above). The graphs are
structurally non-degenerate, with depth for the treatments to exploit.
Discovery is deterministic, so the graphs are stable facts about the data
and not artefacts of a particular run. Disagreement between discovery
methods is kept as a diagnostic rather than averaged away
(Section~\ref{sec:comparators}). Most importantly, the graphs are judged
by the controlled downstream ablation of
Chapters~\ref{chap:sensitivity}--\ref{chap:evaluation}. That is a
task-based test, and it is the only economically meaningful one. I
therefore claim that the graphs are useful and reproducible, not that
they are true.

\section{Controls on the orientation content}
\label{sec:comparators}

The research question hinges on what the edge orientations contribute,
so the design carries two further graph sources that perturb exactly
that ingredient. Both feed the same extraction function, so every
allocator runs unchanged on them.

VARLiNGAM \cite{hyvarinen2010varlingam} supplies differently identified
orientations. Its instantaneous matrix $B_0$ is identified through
non-Gaussianity rather than through a score. LiNGAM's causal order makes
the asset block a DAG by construction. Its identification assumption
(heavy-tailed residuals) weakens mechanically at longer windows. This is
the window fragility that Chapter~\ref{chap:background} anticipated, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} observes it.

Ridge-Granger supplies orientations with degraded magnitudes. It is a
ridge-regularised VAR(1) on the asset panel, computed in closed form,
with $M[i,j] = |{\rm coef}(x_{i,t-1}\!\to\!x_{j,t})|$. I threshold it in
each window to match the edge density of the paired DYNOTEARS graph, so
the comparison is at like-for-like sparsity. Its lagged, shrunken
coefficients still order the assets directionally, but they are not
structural effect sizes. It is also not guaranteed to be acyclic, which
the allocators handle explicitly (a truncated Neumann series for the
total effects, and minimal feedback-arc removal before topological
ordering).

The two controls therefore split ``orientation'' into its parts. If an
effect needs DYNOTEARS' specific arrows, VARLiNGAM should not reproduce
it. If an effect needs trustworthy edge magnitudes rather than arrows
alone, then on ridge-Granger graphs the ordering-based allocators should
survive where the shock-propagation allocators fail. This is the
dissociation that Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} observes. (I also
probed a non-linear extension, NTS-NOTEARS \cite{sun2023ntsnotears}, at
reduced scale, and leave it as future work; see
Section~\ref{sec:future}.)

\section{Engineering for tractability and reproducibility}
\label{sec:cache}

Two pieces of engineering underpin the credibility of every number in
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

\paragraph{The discovery cache.} The graph fit is the dominant cost:
about 4 minutes per window at $d\approx132$, and about 15 hours per
backtest. However, the fit depends only on the window data and the
discovery hyperparameters, not on anything downstream. A content-keyed
disk cache therefore lets every configuration at a given window reuse
one fit. The full ablation grid ran against 1{,}684 cached graph fits
with a verified 100\% hit rate and zero refits, at about 40 seconds per
backtest. The cache also guarantees the ablation's premise. Allocators
cannot see different graphs, because there is exactly one graph per
(method, window, data) key. Appendix~\ref{app:tables} records the cache
mechanics.

\paragraph{A data-determinism defect, found and fixed.} Building the
cache exposed a latent reproducibility bug. One driver series (the
emerging-markets ETF EEM) was silently re-downloaded on every call. The
resulting micro-jitter, amplified by the non-convex DYNOTEARS optimiser,
made the pipeline irreproducible at the bit level. Two conclusions I had
drawn in the project's first phase did not survive the fix. That first
phase was an exploratory precursor on the same panel and protocol. It
evaluated earlier causal allocators, including the skeleton-only
allocator this study anchors on. Its configurations are counted in the
\rsNtrials-trial deflation universe of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, and
its committed skeleton result is the target of the replication gate
described in Section~\ref{sec:ablation-design}. All numbers in this
report come from the corrected, deterministic pipeline. Two independent
runs now produce identical windows, weights and NAVs, verified end-to-end
by that replication gate. A result that does not survive a determinism
fix was never real, so I treat reproducibility as a prerequisite rather
than an afterthought \cite{lopezdeprado2023causal}.
Appendix~\ref{app:tables} records the defect's mechanism, and the
observation that VARLiNGAM's ICA-based fits were robust to the same
jitter.

\chapter{From Graph to Weights}
\label{chap:sensitivity}
% ============================================================

This chapter addresses the second challenge of
Section~\ref{sec:challenges}: getting a hierarchical allocator to use
edge direction at all. Hierarchical allocators take a symmetric distance
matrix. Symmetrising the DAG discards the orientations, and there is no
established way to feed an asymmetric adjacency matrix into clustering or
risk allocation. The output of this chapter is the allocation machinery
that makes the research question answerable. It is a family of
orientation-aware allocators, all fed the identical per-window graph. The
family splits into orientation-blind controls, which can only see the
skeleton, and treatments, which can see the orientations. They are
assembled into a fixed-graph ablation in which orientation is the only
treatment variable. This family is what satisfies the second premise of
Section~\ref{sec:question}: without it, the value of orientation cannot
be measured at all. Section~\ref{sec:symm} states the problem.
Section~\ref{sec:sem} derives the structural quantities the treatments
use. Sections~\ref{sec:controls}--\ref{sec:d-family} define the
allocators, and Section~\ref{sec:ablation-design} assembles them into the
fixed-graph ablation.

\section{The symmetric-clustering problem}
\label{sec:symm}

Hierarchical allocation (Section~\ref{sec:hrp-theme}) takes a symmetric,
non-negative distance matrix. A causal graph is asymmetric by design, and
this asymmetry is the object of study. The naive fix is to symmetrise the
adjacency as $\tfrac{1}{2}(|M| + |M^\top|)$, but this discards the very
orientations the research question asks about. The symmetric clustering
step cannot carry orientation, so the allocation steps themselves must
change. The challenge is therefore not to force $M$ into the existing
interface, but to build allocation steps that take the directed matrix
natively, while changing as little else as possible. That way, any
performance difference is attributable to orientation and nothing else.
The two observations of Section~\ref{sec:hrp-theme} show how. First,
HRP's dendrogram exists only to produce a leaf ordering for recursive
bisection, and a DAG carries a natural ordering for free. Second, HRP's
bisection step takes cluster variances from a covariance matrix, and a
DAG implies one.

\section{The structural model: total effects and the implied covariance}
\label{sec:sem}

Both DYNOTEARS' $W$ and VARLiNGAM's $B_0$, restricted to the asset block
and written in the $i\to j$ convention, define the structural equation
model
\begin{equation}
  (I - M^\top)\, x = \varepsilon
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  x = (I - M^\top)^{-1} \varepsilon \;=:\; B\,\varepsilon,
  \label{eq:sem}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the vector of (standardised) asset returns and $\varepsilon$
is the vector of structural shocks. Because the asset block is a DAG, its
adjacency matrix is nilpotent. So the total-effect matrix
$B = \sum_{k\ge 0} (M^\top)^k$ terminates in at most $N$ terms and exists
exactly, with no spectral-radius assumption. The entry
$B_{ij} = \partial x_i / \partial \varepsilon_j$ aggregates the influence
of a unit shock at asset $j$ on asset $i$ over all directed paths. It is
the Leontief inverse of input--output economics. Its asymmetry is where
orientation enters allocation natively. (For the ridge-Granger control,
which is not a DAG, I approximate $B$ by the truncated series with a
spectral-radius guard.)

The SEM of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sem} implies a covariance. Let
$\Sigma_\varepsilon = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2_{\varepsilon,i})$. The
diagonal form is the SEM's own independent-shocks assumption. Estimating
a dense $\Sigma_\varepsilon$ would reintroduce sample correlation into a
treatment meant to isolate the graph. The SEM-implied covariance in
return units is then
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}
  \;=\; D_\sigma \big( B\, \Sigma_\varepsilon\, B^\top \big) D_\sigma ,
  \label{eq:sigma-sem}
\end{equation}
where the shock variances are estimated from the fit window's structural
residuals $E = X_z (I - M)$, and $D_\sigma$ converts from the $z$-scored
units the graph was fitted in back to return units. The name needs a
comment. $\Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}$ is the covariance of the observables
that the structural model implies (the $\Sigma(\theta)$ of SEM, or the
reduced form in SVAR terms). The structural shocks' own covariance is the
diagonal $\Sigma_\varepsilon$ above. The conversion back to return units
is not optional: recursive bisection compares cluster variances, and
omitting $D_\sigma$ would silently equalise asset volatilities. Two
caveats apply. The asset-block SEM marginalises over the drivers, so
$\varepsilon$ absorbs driver-explained variance and its diagonality is an
approximation. Also, I project $\Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}$ to the nearest
positive semi-definite (PSD) matrix and add a small ridge, so that dense
stress-window graphs cannot produce a singular input.

\section{The controls: correlation and skeleton}
\label{sec:controls}

\paragraph{\HRP{} (the correlation baseline).} This is textbook HRP
\cite{lopezdeprado2016hrp}: the distance
$\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\rho_{ij})}$ on the lookback window's sample
correlation, with the sample covariance for bisection. It is graph-blind,
which I verify by unit test (changing the graph cannot change its
weights), and it anchors the decomposition.

\paragraph{\skelsamp{} (the skeleton control).} The graph replaces the
correlation matrix, but symmetrically. Each asset is embedded by its edge
profile $e_i = [\,M_{i\cdot},\, M_{\cdot i}\,]$, the clustering distance
is $D_{ij} = \lVert e_i - e_j \rVert_2$, and bisection uses the sample
covariance. This embedding is provably invariant to reversing every edge
at once: transposing $M$ swaps the two halves of every $e_i$, which
leaves all pairwise distances, and hence the dendrogram and the weights,
unchanged. A unit test enforces this property. The guarantee is exactly
that and no more. Because $e_i$ keeps outgoing and incoming edges in
separate halves, reversing an individual edge can move mass between
halves and change distances. So \skelsamp{} retains each asset's profile
of source and sink roles, which is information the undirected skeleton
alone does not carry. The control with the stronger guarantee is
\skelalt{} below, and Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} reads the
decomposition against both. \skelsamp{} coincides with the skeleton
allocator I evaluated in the project's first phase
(Section~\ref{sec:cache}), which lets me validate the new harness against
a committed historical result (Section~\ref{sec:ablation-design}).

\paragraph{\skelalt{} (a second symmetrisation).} Hierarchical clustering
demands a symmetric distance and $M$ is not symmetric, so building a
skeleton control requires choosing a symmetrisation, and the choice is
not unique. If the skeleton's measured contribution depended on which
symmetrisation I chose, it would be a fact about the recipe rather than
about the skeleton. \skelalt{} is a control on the control. It is the
same pipeline, but with the $\tfrac{1}{2}(|M|+|M^\top|)$ distance, so
that two assets are close when a strong link joins them rather than when
their edge profiles match. Of the two, \skelalt{} has the stronger
orientation-blindness guarantee. $\tfrac{1}{2}(|M|+|M^\top|)$ is a
function of the undirected weighted adjacency alone, so it is invariant
to reversing any single edge, whereas \skelsamp{}'s embedding is
invariant only to reversing all edges at once. Comparing the two tests
whether ``the skeleton'' has been accidentally identified with one
particular symmetrisation. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} reports where
they disagree.

\section{The orientation-aware family}
\label{sec:d-family}

Section~\ref{sec:symm} left hierarchical allocation with exactly two
moving parts, and that fact bounds the design space. The clustering step
exists only to produce an ordering. The allocation step takes only a
covariance. Nothing else crosses between them: recursive bisection
receives the ordering and the covariance and no third object, and it
splits the ordered list by position rather than by the dendrogram's own
branches. So a causal graph has exactly two entry points into HRP, the
ordering and the covariance. Crossing them enumerates the family
completely (Table~\ref{tab:crossing}).

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{The two entry points, crossed. The row gives where the

    on. \skelsamp{} is the control that takes orientation through
    neither.}
  \label{tab:crossing}
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \toprule
    Ordering from & splits on sample cov. & splits on $\Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}$ \\
    \midrule
    clustering on the skeleton & \skelsamp{} (control) & \skelsem{} \\
    topological sort           & \orientsamp{}           & \orientsem{} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two points about the rows follow. The first row keeps the clustering
step and feeds it a graph-derived distance. The second removes the
clustering step entirely, because a DAG already carries an ordering and
needs no dendrogram to produce one. So what distinguishes the rows is
where the ordering comes from, not what the clustering step reads. Each
treatment differs from the control in exactly one respect: \skelsem{} in
the covariance alone, \orientsamp{} in the ordering alone, and
\orientsem{} in both. This means the results chapter can identify not
just whether orientation carries value but where. All three are
long-only, fully invested, deterministic and seed-free. All take the
identical $(M,\ \text{returns window})$ inputs as the controls. None has
an analogue in the hierarchical-allocation literature.

\paragraph{\skelsem{} (SEM-implied covariance).} Clustering is as in
\skelsamp{}. I deliberately hold the ordering at the skeleton, so that
$\skelsem{}-\skelsamp{}$ isolates orientation's effect on the allocation
step alone. Recursive bisection, however, allocates on
$\Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma-sem} instead of the sample
covariance. Orientation enters through the asymmetric shock propagation
in $B$.

\paragraph{\orientsamp{} / \orientsem{} (causal-ordered bisection).}
\label{sec:d2}
Here the clustering step is removed entirely. HRP's dendrogram exists
only to produce a quasi-diagonalising leaf order, and a DAG supplies an
order natively. I sort the assets from upstream to downstream by a
topological sort of $M$, with ties broken deterministically by total
downstream influence $\sum_r |B_{ri}|$ and then by name. The existing
recursive bisection then runs on that order, with the sample covariance
(\orientsamp{}) or with $\Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}$ (\orientsem{}). This is
the most direct response to the observation that one cannot cluster on
an asymmetric matrix: do not cluster. One subtlety, which the ablation's
own tests identified, should be recorded. Recursive bisection is
mirror-invariant: reversing the leaf order reproduces the identical
partition tree. So \orientsamp{} responds to orientation through the
partition structure the order induces, not through the
upstream/downstream direction itself.

\section{The fixed-graph ablation}
\label{sec:ablation-design}

The experimental design is what turns the family into an answer to the
research question.

\paragraph{Design.} At every rebalance, the extraction function of
Section~\ref{sec:asset-graph} hands the identical graph $M$ to every
allocator in Table~\ref{tab:variants}. Everything else is shared code:
the universe, the calendar, the costs, the covariance-estimation window,
and the allocator internals downstream of the distance, covariance and
ordering choice. So orientation is the sole treatment variable, and the
decomposition is two differences with a common anchor:
$\skelsamp{}-\HRP{}$ (the skeleton) and $\orientany{}-\skelsamp{}$ (the
orientations). The shared anchor cancels when the two are added,
$(\skelsamp{}-\HRP{}) + (\orientany{}-\skelsamp{}) =
\orientany{}-\HRP{}$, so the total causal-graph gain is attributed
without remainder.

The reported grid is 5 graph-consuming allocators $\times$ \{DYNOTEARS,
VARLiNGAM\} $\times$ \{189, 252, 378, 504\}-day windows, plus the
ridge-Granger control, a sparsity sweep and a cost sweep. The evaluated
grid is wider. The original experiment plan contained 7 allocators. I
added the mechanism controls (two direction-free covariance controls and
a graph-free partial-correlation skeleton control), a second hierarchical
family (HERC) and the naive anchors during evaluation. The narrowing to 5
allocators, and the provenance of every extension, are stated in
Section~\ref{sec:believe}, and every evaluated cell is counted in the
deflation universe. The window axis is a design variable in its own
right. The sample moments every allocator uses degrade in opposite
directions as the window shrinks (estimation noise) or grows (staleness
across regimes). So tracing the decomposition over windows from 9 months
to 2 years tests where each kind of graph information adds value. I
report every cell of the grid. At the shortest window, the discovery fit
has $n=189$ observations against $d=132$ variables. This is valid
($n>d$) but thin, and I carry the caveat through to the results.

\paragraph{Verification before results.} Three gates preceded the grid.
The unit-test gate: 60+ tests, written before the grid was run. These
include the orientation-blindness of the controls, the
orientation-sensitivity of every treatment, total-effect and
SEM-covariance identities against hand-computed cases, and
topological-order properties on random DAGs. The cache gate: every graph
fit is verified as a cache hit with zero refits, so no allocator can have
seen a different graph. The replication gate: \skelsamp{}, run through
the new harness, exactly reproduces the committed first-phase bundle.
This establishes both that the new machinery is faithful and that the
pipeline is deterministic end-to-end. Appendix~\ref{app:tables} records
the gates' figures.

\chapter{Decomposing the Gain}
\label{chap:evaluation}
% ============================================================

This chapter addresses the third challenge of
Section~\ref{sec:challenges}: statistical inference at realistic effect
sizes. The effects at stake are of the order of 0.01 to 0.03 Sharpe on
about 18 years of data, spread across many evaluated configurations. A
pairwise $p$-value means nothing there unless I account for the
multiplicity, quantify the power, and control for non-causal mechanisms
such as shrinkage and market-factor removal. The output is a
pre-registered, controlled, multiplicity-aware evaluation. It uses a
block bootstrap, the Deflated Sharpe ratio, SPA and MCS (including a
pooled SPA), a minimum detectable effect, pre-committed controls and an
out-of-sample slice. The chapter follows the order in which the research
question must be answered. Section~\ref{sec:method} fixes how I measure
the gain. Section~\ref{sec:r1} tests the first premise, whether the
causal graph improves on the correlation matrix at all.
Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} presents the decomposition of the gain
into skeleton and orientation, which is the answer to the research
question, together with its robustness to the design's free parameters.
Section~\ref{sec:believe-rigour} subjects every claim to the
multiplicity-aware battery, and Section~\ref{sec:believe} closes with the
threats to validity.

\section{Measurement protocol}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Protocol.} Every configuration runs the identical walk-forward
backtest: the fixed 99-name universe, monthly rebalancing over
2007--2024, estimation windows of 189, 252, 378 and 504 trading days for
both the graph fit and the sample covariance, weights held for 21 trading
days, and transaction costs of 5\,bps per unit of one-way turnover
(swept over $\{0,5,10,20\}$\,bps). There are 215 rebalances at windows up
to 1 year; the longer burn-ins of the 378- and 504-day windows leave 209
and 203. ``Performance'' always means the out-of-sample, net-of-cost,
annualised Sharpe ratio of daily portfolio returns. I report it alongside
CAGR, maximum drawdown and mean turnover (Table~\ref{tab:return-terms} in
Appendix~\ref{app:tables} tabulates these for the headline
configurations). No in-sample number appears anywhere in this report,
and gross figures appear only as the zero-cost arm of the cost sweep.

\paragraph{Inference.} Pairwise contrasts use the Politis--Romano
stationary block bootstrap \cite{politis1994stationary} with 10{,}000
resamples and a mean block length of 21 days. It resamples the joint
return panel, so cross-strategy correlation is preserved. Daily returns
here are heavily non-normal (excess kurtosis ${\approx}\,\rsKurtosis$;
Figure~\ref{fig:retdist}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}), and I evaluated
\rsNtrials\ configurations in total. (The deflation universe
deliberately keeps byte-identical duplicates, which is the conservative
choice.) So I never take pairwise $p$-values at face value. Every claim
is re-tested by the battery of Section~\ref{sec:believe-rigour}: the
Probabilistic and Deflated Sharpe ratios
\cite{bailey2012psr,bailey2014deflated}, with the DSR deflating against
all \rsNtrials\ trials; Hansen's SPA \cite{hansen2005spa}, the
studentised successor of White's Reality Check \cite{white2000reality},
for each family-wise comparison; and the $90\%$ Model Confidence Set
\cite{hansen2011mcs}. Regimes are defined by NBER recession dates and by
the top and bottom VIX quintiles.

\paragraph{Stability.} Every allocator in the study is deterministic and
seed-free. Two independent end-to-end runs produce identical graphs,
weights and NAVs, a property verified by the replication gate
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation-design}). Every number in this chapter comes
from the pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:cache}.

\section{Premise 1: the causal graph against the correlation matrix}
\label{sec:r1}

The first premise asks whether there is any gain to decompose. The
like-for-like comparison is the graph-blind \HRP{} baseline against the
causal-graph allocators on the same machinery, at all 4 windows. The
baseline itself earns $\rsCorrSharpeWone$, $\rsCorrSharpe$,
$\rsCorrSharpeWthree$ and $\rsCorrSharpeWfive$ at the 189-, 252-, 378-
and 504-day windows. The decomposition's reference treatment is
\skelsem{}, which differs from the skeleton control in the allocation
step alone. It delivers a total causal-graph gain over the baseline of
$+0.018$, $+0.031$, $+0.030$ and $+0.032$ respectively
($p=0.43$--$0.11$; Table~\ref{tab:decomp}), and \orientsem{} reaches
$+0.036$ at 504 days. There are 40 graph-versus-\HRP{} cells (5
graph-consuming allocators, both discovery methods, 4 windows), and 36 of
them are above the baseline. Under DYNOTEARS it is 19 of 20; the single
exception is the skeleton-only allocator at the 189-day window.

This consistency should be read as robustness, not as significance. The
cells share one return panel, overlapping windows and common machinery,
so they are far from independent draws, and no sign-count converts into
a valid $p$-value. What it establishes is that the direction of the gain
does not depend on the choice of allocator, discovery method or window.
No cell is individually significant, and I leave inference under the
panel's dependence structure to the family-wise tests of
Section~\ref{sec:believe-rigour}. In brief, Hansen's SPA rejects nowhere
over the full allocator grid, and crosses the $5\%$ bar only at the 2
longest windows under a narrowed family that was fixed after these
results were seen. So the first premise rests on the sign consistency
and the stable total, not on family-wise significance: the causal graph
outperforms the correlation matrix by a modest margin at every window.
The decomposition that follows does not stand or fall with this premise
in any case. It compares the graph-fed allocators against each other on
identical inputs, so it remains informative about where any gain,
whatever its size, comes from.

In return terms, the gain is $+0.4$ to $+0.7$ percentage points of net
CAGR per year ($5.62\%$ against $4.97\%$ for \skelsem{} versus the
baseline at 252 days; Table~\ref{tab:return-terms}). Two naive anchors
bound the absolute levels. Equal weight earns $\rsEwSharpe$ and inverse
variance earns $\rsIvpSharpe$ at 252 days
(Table~\ref{tab:return-terms}). So every member of the hierarchical
family clears $1/N$ at every window, though the correlation baseline
itself trails inverse variance at 3 of the 4 windows. The thesis's
claims are contrasts within the family; the anchors show that the
family's absolute edge on this universe is modest.

\section{The decomposition: skeleton versus orientation}
\label{sec:decomposition}

\subsection{The decomposition across estimation windows}

Figure~\ref{fig:gradient} is the thesis's central result: the two
components of the decomposition traced across the 4 estimation windows.
Table~\ref{tab:decomp} gives the numbers, Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} the
full allocator-by-method matrix, and Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}
(Appendix~\ref{app:tables}) the per-window levels. Three features carry
the answer to the research question. First, the total causal-graph gain
is stable: $+0.018$ to $+0.032$ for \skelsem{} at every window, rising
to $+0.036$ for \orientsem{} at 504 days. Second, the skeleton
contribution is hump-shaped ($-0.003$ at 189 days, peaking at $+0.022$
at 252 days, decaying to $+0.009$ at 504 days), and it is never
individually significant ($p \geq 0.14$). Third, the orientation
contribution is positive at every window and U-shaped ($+0.021$ at
$p=0.08$, $+0.009$ at $p=0.45$, $+0.012$ at $p=0.31$, $+0.023$ at
$p=0.048$). It is largest at the two extremes and smallest where the
skeleton peaks, and 11 of the 12 DYNOTEARS orientation contrasts across
the grid are positive (Figure~\ref{fig:forest}). The two components are
roughly complementary, which is why the total is flat while its
composition shifts. Family-wise, the orientation-aware allocators
against the shared skeleton control reject only at 504 days, where the
pairwise effect also peaks, and only under the narrowed family.
Section~\ref{sec:believe-rigour} reports both families and sizes that
rejection.

\paragraph{Anchor-dependence of the shapes.} The contributions above are
measured against \skelsamp{}. Measuring them against \skelalt{} instead,
the symmetrisation with the stronger orientation-blindness guarantee
(Section~\ref{sec:controls}), changes them. The skeleton contribution
becomes flat ($+0.022$, $+0.018$, $+0.024$, $+0.019$ across the 4
windows). The orientation contribution becomes
$-0.003$/$+0.013$/$+0.006$/$+0.012$ for \skelsem{} and
$-0.003$/$+0.008$/$+0.010$/$+0.016$ for \orientsem{} (point estimates,
not formally tested). The two anchors agree that orientation's increment
is positive at every window from 1 year out, and largest at the longer
windows. They disagree at 189 days, where \skelalt{} beats \skelsamp{}
by $+0.025$ ($p=0.013$), the strongest control-versus-control divergence
in the study. So at the shortest window, the choice of symmetrisation
matters as much as orientation itself, and I read the 189-day column
with that caveat throughout. The hump and the U are descriptions
relative to \skelsamp{}. The findings that hold under both anchors are
the flat-to-positive skeleton gain and the orientation increment at long
estimation windows.

\subsection{What orientation contributes}
\label{sec:orientation-role}

Orientation contributes robustness to the estimation window rather than
a higher level of performance. The skeleton allocator's performance is
window-fragile ($0.372$--$0.403$ across the 4 windows, and below the
baseline at 189 days). \skelsem{} ($0.395$--$0.415$) and \orientsem{}
($0.399$--$0.419$) are strong everywhere (Figure~\ref{fig:nav} shows the
252-day NAV paths). The U-shape of Figure~\ref{fig:gradient} indicates
when orientation pays: when the sample moments feeding the allocator are
least reliable. At 189 days the sample covariance is estimated from
barely $1.9$ observations per asset (noise). At 504 days it averages
across regimes it should distinguish (staleness). At the 252-day window,
where the estimation literature puts the optimum between noise and
staleness, and where the skeleton's own contribution peaks, orientation
adds least. $\Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}$ is built from the contemporaneous
graph and its shock variances, so it substitutes structure for
sample-moment quality at both ends of that trade-off. On this reading,
the SEM covariance acts as a regulariser of the allocation step rather
than as a source of extra return. The controls at the end of this
subsection identify which of its properties does the regularising, and
the answer is not direction. Two qualifications bound the mechanism.
First, the window moves the graph and the sample moments together (both
are estimated on the same lookback), so the gradient cannot by itself
separate degradation of the moments from degradation of the graph. The
stability of the discovered graphs across windows (density
$6.3$--$6.9\%$ at every window; Section~\ref{sec:asset-graph}) supports
the reading that the moments degrade faster. Second, the 189-day arm of
the U depends on the anchor (Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}), so I claim
the regulariser mechanism only for the long-window arm.

\paragraph{An alternative explanation, tested directly.} The contrast
$\skelsem{}-\skelsamp{}$ compares the SEM-implied covariance against the
sample covariance. However, $\Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}$ differs from the
sample covariance in more ways than direction. It is a structured
estimator with far fewer effective parameters, so it behaves like
shrinkage. And its independent-shock assumption strips out the common
market component. Either property is achievable with no graph at all,
so a sceptic could read the gain from the covariance step as generic
shrinkage or as de-factoring. I test both directly. I wrote down and
committed the interpretation of every possible outcome before either
control was run (Section~\ref{sec:believe}). Both controls keep the
skeleton allocator's clustering fixed and replace its sample covariance:
the first with a Ledoit--Wolf shrunk covariance \cite{ledoitwolf2004},
and the second with a de-factored covariance, in which each asset is
regressed on the window's equal-weight market return and allocation
uses the residual covariance. Neither control sees an edge direction.

The shrinkage account fails. The Ledoit--Wolf control sits below the
plain skeleton allocator at every window ($\rsDlwSharpeWone$,
$\rsDlwSharpe$, $\rsDlwSharpeWthree$, $\rsDlwSharpeWfive$ across the 4
windows), and \skelsem{} beats it by $+0.018$ to $+0.042$ ($p=0.05$ at
504 days, its strongest window; $p \geq 0.08$ elsewhere). The
de-factoring account succeeds. The de-factored control
($\rsDdfSharpeWone$, $\rsDdfSharpe$, $\rsDdfSharpeWthree$,
$\rsDdfSharpeWfive$) reproduces the covariance-step gain at every
window, leaving a residual of \skelsem{} minus control between $-0.011$
and $+0.004$ ($p \geq 0.5$ throughout). Following the reading I
committed in advance, I do not attribute the covariance-step gain to
directional content. The operative ingredient of $\Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}$
is its independent-shock assumption, which removes the common market
component, and that removal needs no graph. What survives for the
orientations is a weaker, conditional role. DYNOTEARS magnitudes recover
the full de-factoring benefit without damaging it. The identical
construction on VARLiNGAM graphs, which are equally structured and
equally de-factored, yields nothing, and the ridge-Granger version, with
its degraded magnitudes, collapses (both are shown in the
discovery-method comparison of the next subsection). So trustworthy edge
magnitudes are necessary for the construction to work, but the working
ingredient is the de-factoring, not the direction of the edges.

\paragraph{The skeleton, given the same treatment.} The covariance
controls discipline the orientation gain, and symmetry demands the same
scepticism of the skeleton gain. Its gain over \HRP{} ($+0.022$ at 252
days) could in principle be reproduced by any sparse
conditional-dependence structure of the same density, with no discovery
at all. A third control tests this directly, again with interpretations
committed before it was run
(\texttt{PREDICTIONS\_SKELETON\_CONTROL.md}). It is \skelsamp{}'s
construction with the discovered skeleton replaced by a thresholded
partial-correlation matrix. I invert a Ledoit--Wolf covariance to get a
precision matrix, keep the largest-magnitude cells, and match the
density to the paired graph's nonzero-cell count. The graph enters only
through that scalar. The graph-free skeleton fails to reproduce the gain
exactly where the gain is. At 252 days it earns $\rsDpcSharpe$ against
the discovered skeleton's $0.403$, a residual of $+0.025$ ($p=0.008$),
and its own gain over the correlation baseline is zero there ($-0.003$,
$p=0.86$). At the other 3 windows the two skeletons are statistically
indistinguishable (residuals within $\pm 0.004$, $p \geq 0.69$), and
those are the windows where the skeleton contributes little anyway.
Following the committed reading, I attribute the skeleton's gain at 252
days to the discovered skeleton itself. The mechanism story is therefore
asymmetric in an informative way. The orientation gain in the covariance
step needs no graph, because de-factoring reproduces it. The skeleton's
252-day gain does need the graph.

\subsection{Robustness to design parameters}

\paragraph{Sparsity ($\tau$).} Thresholding the graph at
$\tau\in\{0,0.01,0.05,0.10\}$ leaves the treatment flat to slightly
better (\orientsamp{}: $0.399\to0.402$ at the heaviest threshold), while
the skeleton control drifts down ($0.403\to0.394$;
Table~\ref{tab:tau}). So the orientation-aware allocators are not
artefacts of the regularisation threshold. These sweep differences are
point estimates; I did not test them formally.

\paragraph{Costs and turnover.} The gain from the covariance step is
unchanged by costs, at least in point estimates.
\skelsem{}$-$\skelsamp{} at 504 days moves from $+0.022$ to $+0.025$ as
costs rise from 0 to 20\,bps (differences on unrounded series;
Table~\ref{tab:cost}). This is because \skelsem{}'s turnover ($0.102$
one-way per rebalance) is below the control's ($0.112$). The claim does
not extend to the ordering route at short windows. \orientsem{} has the
family's highest turnover ($0.140$), and its 252-day increment erodes
from $+0.004$ at zero cost to $0.000$ at 20\,bps, though its 504-day
increment survives the sweep. So the covariance step does not win by
trading less and is not eroded by trading more, whereas the ordering
step at 252 days is.

\paragraph{Discovery method.} Under VARLiNGAM graphs the orientation
effect is absent at all 4 windows (4 of 12 contrasts positive, mean
$-0.005$; Figure~\ref{fig:forest}, right, and the grey series of
Figure~\ref{fig:gradient}). So the decomposition depends on the
discovery method. DYNOTEARS' jointly optimised, acyclicity-constrained
$W$ supports orientation-aware allocation, and VARLiNGAM's ICA-ordered
$B_0$ does not. This is consistent with VARLiNGAM's identification
weakening as residuals become more Gaussian over longer windows. At the
189-day window it is also consistent with its unpruned $B_0$ saturating
in the thin-sample regime (density $32\%$ against DYNOTEARS' stable
$6$--$7\%$; Section~\ref{sec:asset-graph}), so its short-window graphs
carry little usable orientation signal either.

A DYNOTEARS graph supplies two things at once, the direction of each
edge and a fitted magnitude for it, so its cells alone cannot show which
of the two the orientation effect uses. The ridge-Granger control
separates them: it preserves a usable ordering while degrading the
magnitudes. On its graphs, causal-ordered bisection survives
(\orientsamp{} $=0.393$ against DYNOTEARS' $0.399$), while the
SEM-covariance allocator collapses (\skelsem{} $=0.352$; a gap of
$-0.060$, $p=0.11$). A ridge-VAR coefficient matrix carries a
serviceable ordering but not structural effect sizes. So the
dissociation indicates that orientation's contribution does not lie in
merely knowing which way the arrows point (an ordering survives even a
one-lag ridge regression). It lies in edge magnitudes trustworthy enough
to propagate shocks, which only the jointly optimised DYNOTEARS fit
supplies. This refines the answer to the research question rather than
adding a separate benchmark: ``orientation'' pays through its weighted,
propagatable form.

\paragraph{A second family member (HERC).} Chapter~\ref{chap:background}
argued that the limitation is family-wide: any hierarchical allocator
that clusters on a symmetric matrix discards orientation, whatever rule
it uses to read the tree. Whether the measured decomposition generalises
across tree-reading rules is a separate, empirical question. I test it
on a second member, with interpretations committed before any cell was
run (\texttt{PREDICTIONS\_HERC.md}). The member is a full-recursion
Hierarchical Equal Risk Contribution allocator \cite{raffinot2018herc}.
It is identical to the HRP family in its clustering step and its
cluster-risk measure, but it descends the dendrogram's actual topology
instead of bisecting the leaf ordering. The outcome is mixed and bounds
the generalisation. The orientation contribution's sign carries at every
window, the skeleton contribution's sign does not, and the whole HERC
family sits well below its HRP counterparts (cells and contrasts in
Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). So the decomposition's magnitudes are
allocator-specific. What generalises to this second member is
orientation's sign, and I keep the family-wide claim only in its
structural form. All 12 HERC cells join the deflation universe and
neither SPA family.

\subsection{Regime dependence}
\label{sec:regimes}

The prior-verification result (Section~\ref{sec:discovery-run}) showed
that edge assignment matters most in stress windows. This motivated the
hypothesis that orientation-aware allocation would add most value in
crises. The regime-conditional analysis contradicts this. At the 252-day
window, the orientation-aware allocators' edge over the \HRP{} baseline
concentrates in the bottom VIX quintile ($+0.29$/$+0.31$ Sharpe for
\skelsem{}/\orientsem{}) and is slightly negative in the top quintile.
In NBER recessions the skeleton allocator is the strongest
(Figure~\ref{fig:regime} and Table~\ref{tab:regime-full},
Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). These figures are descriptive. I ran no
formal regime-level test, and conditional Sharpe levels are mechanically
inflated in the calm regime, so only within-regime differences should be
read. A plausible reading is that in stress, correlations rise towards
one and any structure is swamped by the common factor, whereas in calm
markets cross-sectional causal heterogeneity is the signal. Whatever the
mechanism, the hypothesis as stated was wrong. Howard et al.'s finding
that their centrality alpha lives in stable regimes
(Section~\ref{sec:howard-theme}) had anticipated this outcome.

allocator's Probabilistic Sharpe ratio, the probability that its true

equivalence test lacks the power to rank the causal family, including
separating treatments from controls.

\paragraph{Verdict on the research question, with uncertainty stated.}
The decomposition's point estimates are clear and internally consistent.
There is a stable total gain whose composition shifts with the
estimation window: the skeleton is hump-shaped and peaks at 252 days,
and orientation is positive at every window and largest at the extremes.
The orientation-aware allocators top the study-wide DSR table. The
statistical support is modest, and I report it at its estimated size.
36 of 40 graph-versus-correlation cells are positive. One orientation
cell among 12 is pairwise significant ($p=0.048$). There is no
family-wise rejection anywhere under the full grid. There are marginal
rejections at the longest estimation windows under the post-hoc
narrowed family only, coinciding with the peaks of the point estimates.
And there is no rejection for either family once the pooled test
accounts for the window search alongside the strategy search
(Table~\ref{tab:spa}). The mechanism controls of
Section~\ref{sec:orientation-role} land asymmetrically. The orientation
gain in the covariance step is reproduced by a graph-free de-factored
covariance, so it is robustness, not directional information as such.
The skeleton's 252-day gain is not reproduced by a graph-free skeleton
of matched density, so the gain at the conventional window does need the
discovered graph. The largest defensible claim is modest. Replacing the
correlation matrix with a DYNOTEARS graph pays through the skeleton near
the 1-year window, and keeping the orientations costs nothing while
improving robustness to the choice of estimation window. The evidence
does not support attributing the covariance-step gain to edge direction,
any orientation effect under VARLiNGAM graphs, or the stress hypothesis.
That is the finding: precise, sign-consistent point estimates whose
family-wise support appears only under the post-hoc narrowing, and whose
leading mechanism a direction-free control reproduces.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:believe}

\paragraph{Effect sizes sit near the detection floor.} The
decomposition's components are $0.01$--$0.03$ Sharpe over 18 years,
against a full-sample Sharpe standard error of
${\approx}\,\rsSharpeSEFull$ (Lo's approximation on the $\sim$4{,}500-day
sample). The pairwise contrasts are far more precise, because the
strategies are highly correlated and the bootstrap differences the joint
panel. The block-bootstrap standard error of the total contrast
(\skelsem{}$-$\HRP{} at 252 days) is $\rsContrastSETotal$, and that of
the orientation contrast (\orientsem{}$-$\skelsamp{}) is
$\rsContrastSEOrient$. Those standard errors put numbers on
``underpowered''. At $5\%$ size and $80\%$ power, the one-sided minimum
detectable effects are ${\approx}\,\rsContrastMDETotal$ and
${\approx}\,\rsContrastMDEOrient$ net Sharpe respectively, roughly twice
the observed $+0.031$ total and $+0.023$ orientation increments. So the
design could only have certified effects about double the size actually
observed. That cuts both ways: the rejections that do occur are marginal
and narrowed-family only, and the absence of rejection at the shorter
windows is not evidence of absence. At the pairwise level, one $p=0.048$
cell among 12 orientation contrasts is close to what multiplicity
predicts under the null. That is why I claim no single cell as a
discovery. The orientation effect earns its standing from the sign
consistency of the contribution across windows and treatments
(Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}), not from any single cell.

\paragraph{Design choices informed by earlier results.} Three design
choices were informed by earlier results, and this paragraph records
them. The first is the choice of question. It was informed by the
results of the project's first phase, which took Hierarchical
Sensitivity Parity \cite{rodriguez2023hsp} (Section~\ref{sec:hrp-theme})
as its starting point and replaced the method's correlation-based driver
selection with selection from the discovered graph. That phase
contributes \rsNtrialsPhaseI\ of the \rsNtrials\ configurations in the
deflation universe, including the two skeleton cells that the
replication gate of Section~\ref{sec:ablation-design} targets. The graph
route of this study contributes the remaining \rsNtrialsPhaseII.
Appendix~\ref{app:hsp} records the first phase's two null results. The
second is the window grid, which reached its 4-window form in two
stages. I evaluated the conventional 252- and 504-day windows first, and
added the 189- and 378-day windows when the initial results suggested a
composition shift that a two-point design cannot distinguish from noise.
Three disciplines bound the associated risk. I ran the added windows
symmetrically (every allocator, both methods, all cells reported). The
deflation universe covers all \rsNtrials\ configurations ever evaluated,
in both phases and at all windows. And the headline reading (orientation
positive everywhere, largest at the extremes) would have been weakened,
and reported as such, had the added windows come back inconsistent. The
narrowed-family rejections inherit the risk unevenly. The strongest
(Premise 1 at 378 days, $p=\rsSpaSkelWthree$) sits on an added window.
The 504-day rejections ($p=\rsSpaSkelWfive$, $\rsSpaDirWfive$) sit on
windows evaluated from the start, which bounds the concern for them.

The third informed choice is the family itself, and it is the most
consequential. The plan's grid contained 7 allocators. I narrowed it to
the 5 allocators of the two-entry-point taxonomy of
Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity} after the 4-window results had been seen.
The taxonomy is a principled reason for the smaller family. The thesis
question is about HRP's two entry points, and the two excluded arms
enter the graph elsewhere: both are further orientation-consuming
allocators, one replacing the hierarchy with risk parity over structural
shocks, the other building the clustering distance from shared upstream
ancestry. However, the timing means the narrowed family's rejections
cannot be read as confirmatory, and this is exactly the choice the
significance turns on. I therefore report every family-wise number for
both families in Table~\ref{tab:spa}, where the full grid rejects
nowhere on the window axis and the narrowed family supplies every
rejection. The two excluded arms stay in the deflation universe. Their
results are directionally consistent with the family they were excluded
from: above the baseline in all 16 of their cells, across both discovery
methods and all 4 windows, by $+0.002$ to $+0.024$. So the exclusion is
not results-driven in direction, but the reader should weigh the
narrowed-family rejections with the timing in view. Separately, I added
the two mechanism controls of Section~\ref{sec:orientation-role} after
this chapter's results were first drafted, in response to the objection
that $\Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}$'s gain might be generic. Their
interpretations were written down and committed before either control
was run, and they join the deflation universe but neither family.

\paragraph{Single asset class, one universe, approximated index.}
Everything here concerns long-only allocation within ${\sim}100$ US
large-caps, and every variant absorbs the full ${\approx}50\%$ GFC
drawdown. Whether the decomposition transfers to universes with richer
exogenous structure (multi-asset, credit, commodities) is untested. The
top-by-market-cap approximation of the S\&P~100, the fixed
snapshot-union universe (whose membership is partly settled by market
caps realised later in the sample), and the absence of delisting returns
(Section~\ref{sec:data}) carry mild residual survivorship and look-ahead
effects. Their sign is favourable, but every allocator on the same panel
shares them, so they inflate the absolute levels rather than the
pairwise contrasts the thesis is about. Quantifying them with a
point-in-time universe is left to future work.

\paragraph{Method-dependence is a caveat on the mechanism.} The
orientation effect exists under DYNOTEARS graphs only. Until a second
discovery method reproduces it, the safest interpretation is that
DYNOTEARS' contemporaneous structure supports orientation-aware
allocation, not that causal orientation in markets is valuable for
allocation in general. The verdict of Section~\ref{sec:believe-rigour}
stands as this chapter's summary.

\section{Summary of findings}
\label{sec:summary}

This thesis asked one question: when the correlation matrix in
hierarchical portfolio allocation is replaced by a causal graph, is
there a performance improvement, and how much of any improvement is
attributable to the graph's skeleton versus its edge orientations? To
make the question answerable, I built the machinery it presupposes. The
first part is a reproducible rolling discovery pipeline that delivers
the identical asset-to-asset DAG to every allocator
(Chapter~\ref{chap:discovery}). The second is a family of
orientation-aware allocators, with skeleton and correlation controls
verified orientation-blind under stated invariances
(Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity}); this satisfies the second premise of
Section~\ref{sec:question}. I then answered the question under inference
that accounts for multiplicity, non-normality and stability
(Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}).

In summary, the causal graph's gain over the correlation matrix is
stable across estimation windows, but its source shifts. Against the
primary skeleton control, the skeleton pays most clearly near the
conventional 1-year window, while orientation pays at every window and,
whichever orientation-blind control I use, from 1 year out. The
between-window differences are about one standard error, and I widened
the window grid partway through, so the shapes carry that uncertainty.
Orientation buys robustness to the estimation window, not a higher level
of performance. A direction-free de-factored covariance reproduces that
robustness, so the mechanism is market-factor removal rather than
directional content. Family-wise, nothing rejects over the full
allocator grid of every strategy the experiment plan evaluated. Support
reaches conventional significance only under the narrower reported
family, at the longest estimation windows, marginally, where the point
estimates also peak. In detail (net annualised Sharpe, 2007--2024,
monthly rebalancing, 5\,bps costs, windows of 189/252/378/504 trading
days):

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item The total gain is stable across estimation windows. Replacing
        the correlation matrix with the DYNOTEARS graph is worth $+0.018$
        to $+0.032$ net Sharpe (\skelsem{} $-$ \HRP{}) at every window,
        and 36 of 40 graph-versus-baseline cells are positive.
  \item Its composition shifts with the window. The skeleton's
        contribution is hump-shaped and peaks at 252 days. Orientation's
        is positive everywhere and U-shaped, largest where the sample
        moments are weakest. Orientation buys robustness to the
        estimation window, not level, and at the 378-day window
        \orientsem{} has the highest Deflated Sharpe ratio of all
        \rsNtrials\ configurations evaluated ($\rsDtwosDSRWthree$).
  \item The orientation mechanism needs no graph; the skeleton does. A
        de-factored covariance with no graph reproduces the gain from
        the covariance step at every window, so the orientation
        robustness works through market-factor removal. The matching
        graph-free skeleton control fails to reproduce the skeleton's
        252-day gain, so the component that pays at the conventional
        window does need the discovered graph
        (Section~\ref{sec:orientation-role}). The shapes themselves
        depend on the anchor at 189 days (limitation (vii) below).
  \item Family-wise support depends on the family, and the full grid
        does not reject. Every SPA test runs over both the full
        7-allocator grid and the narrowed 5-allocator family fixed after
        the results were seen. The full grid rejects nowhere. The
        narrowed family rejects marginally at the longest estimation
        windows only. The pooled test that accounts for the strategy and
        window searches jointly rejects for neither family
        (Table~\ref{tab:spa}). The $90\%$ Model Confidence Set does not
        separate treatments from controls.
  \item The effect is method- and allocator-specific. Under VARLiNGAM
        graphs orientation adds nothing at any window (limitation (ii)).
        Under the ridge-Granger control, which degrades the magnitudes,
        the ordering survives but the SEM covariance collapses, so the
        value sits in trustworthy edge magnitudes. On a second family
        member (HERC) only orientation's sign generalises
        (Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}). Descriptively, the edge
        concentrates in calm regimes, and the stress hypothesis is
        rejected.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The contribution of this thesis is the orientation-aware allocator
family and the fixed-graph ablation design. The decomposition of
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} is what that instrument found. That single
claim unpacks into a methodological core and two strands that flow from
it:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Methodological (the core). I built the orientation-aware
        allocator family: allocation on the SEM-implied covariance,
        which is de-standardised and calibrated on the fit window's
        residuals, and causal-ordered bisection, which replaces the
        clustering dendrogram with the DAG's topological order. I paired
        this with the fixed-graph ablation design, which makes the
        skeleton and the orientations separately measurable, with
        controls verified orientation-blind under stated invariances. To
        my knowledge, both the family and the decomposition are new. The
        closest prior work \cite{howard2025causal} never sets weights
        from its graphs.
  \item Empirical (what the instrument found). I traced the
        decomposition across 4 estimation windows. The total is stable.
        Against the primary anchor, the skeleton is hump-shaped and
        peaks at 252 days, and orientation is positive at every window
        (and positive under both anchors from 1 year out). Family-wise
        support is absent under the full grid, and marginal and confined
        to the longest windows under the narrowed family. I identified
        the mechanism behind the covariance-step gain as market-factor
        removal by a direct control, and I characterised the
        decomposition's dependence on discovery method and regime.
  \item Meta-scientific. I built a reproducible, fully deterministic
        pipeline. Along the way I found and fixed a data-determinism
        defect whose fix overturned two previously drawn conclusions.
        Every design extension is stated as such, every cell is
        cache-verified, and the harness is gated on an exact replication
        of the prior phase's result.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

(i) The decomposition's components are $0.01$--$0.03$ Sharpe. That is
near the 18-year detection floor, individually insignificant against
the stronger controls, and quantifiably underpowered. The one-sided
minimum detectable effects at $80\%$ power are
${\approx}\,\rsContrastMDETotal$ (total) and
${\approx}\,\rsContrastMDEOrient$ (orientation) net Sharpe, roughly
twice the observed increments (Section~\ref{sec:believe}). The point
estimates are exactly reproducible. The inference, where it clears the
family-wise bar, is marginal, clears it only under the post-hoc narrowed
family and not under the full grid, and does not survive accounting for
the window search jointly with the strategy search.
(ii) The orientation effect exists under one discovery method
(DYNOTEARS). Until another method reproduces it, it should be read as a
property of DYNOTEARS' contemporaneous structure, not of markets. The
de-factored control shows that its covariance-step gain is reproducible
with no graph at all, whereas the matching control shows that the
skeleton's 252-day gain is not.
(iii) The study covers long-only US large-cap equity only. Every variant
absorbs the full ${\approx}50\%$ GFC drawdown, and transfer to richer
universes is untested.
(iv) The universe approximates the S\&P~100 by market-cap ranking and a
fixed snapshot union, with mild residual survivorship effects.
(v) The asset-block SEM marginalises over the macro drivers, so the
assumed independence of the structural shocks is an approximation.
(vi) Three design choices were informed by earlier results. The choice
of research question was informed by the results of the first phase
(the Hierarchical Sensitivity Parity route, \rsNtrialsPhaseI\ of the
\rsNtrials\ trials). The window grid was widened to 4 estimation windows
partway through the study. And the family-wise comparison set was
narrowed from the full 7-allocator grid to 5 after the results were seen
(Section~\ref{sec:believe}). All three are stated, both families'
numbers are reported, and the unified \rsNtrials-trial deflation
universe counts every configuration the search ever touched. However, a
deflation correction is not a substitute for out-of-sample replication.
The 2025--26 slice (Section~\ref{sec:oos}), whose protocol was fixed
before the data existed, supplies the directional part of that
replication: every sign it specified came back consistent. At 19 months
it can certify signs and nothing more, so the significance question
stays open.
(vii) At the shortest window the two orientation-blind controls
themselves disagree (\skelalt{} $-$ \skelsamp{} $= +0.025$, $p=0.013$),
so the 189-day contributions carry extra estimator uncertainty. More
generally, the hump and U shapes of the decomposition are measured
against \skelsamp{}. Against \skelalt{}, the skeleton contribution is
flat and the 189-day orientation contribution is slightly negative
(Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}).
(viii) I did not quantify month-to-month persistence of the discovered
edges. Graph stability over time is supported by the determinism of the
fits and the stability of aggregate diagnostics, not by an edge-overlap
measure.

\section{Broader impact}
\label{sec:impact}

\paragraph{For investors.} Allocation research is used by asset managers
and pension schemes, so its failures are borne, diffusely and years
later, by end savers. The characteristic harm in this literature is not
a malfunctioning system. It is a convincing backtest of an overfit
strategy. So evaluation that corrects for multiplicity and reports
family-wise results is not methodological decoration. It is how research
method protects investors, and I apply it to this report's own
headline. The same discipline corrects what might be called
causal-washing. A ``causal'' label invites over-trust, and the finding
here is that a causal method's value can be modest, window- and
method-dependent, and, at the conventional 1-year window, largely
attributable to structure that cheap symmetrisation already captures.
The label guarantees nothing. The decomposition, not the branding, is
what carries information.

\paragraph{For the field.} One of this project's technical choices
generalises into a norm. The determinism episode of
Section~\ref{sec:cache}, in which a reproducibility fix overturned two
conclusions, argues that reproducibility belongs among publication
prerequisites rather than aspirations. The deterministic, seed-free
construction of the allocator family is the design corollary, and it is
what makes every number in this report exactly replicable.

\paragraph{Costs.} The content-keyed cache cut the marginal cost of a
configuration from ${\sim}15$ hours to ${\sim}40$ seconds. That is what
made a \rsNtrials-configuration study, and hence its multiplicity
correction, affordable at all. The compute and energy economics of
caching-first experimental design are a transferable, if unglamorous,
contribution to sustainable empirical research. Rigour and efficiency
were complements here, not a trade-off.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

(1) A second orientation source. The decomposition's biggest caveat is
method-dependence. Repeating the fixed-graph ablation on graphs from a
different family would test whether orientation value is a DYNOTEARS
artefact. The non-linear NTS-NOTEARS is the natural candidate. I
verified in a reduced-scope probe that the directional prior transfers
to it, but its ${\sim}10\times$ fit cost deferred a full backtest.
(2) Signed structural allocation. Equalising risk contributions over
structural shock origins (where risk enters the causal system), rather
than over correlated returns, is the natural next use of
$\Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}$. A long-only variant was among the 7 allocators
of the pre-registered grid and is counted in the full-grid tests of
Section~\ref{sec:believe-rigour}. However, exact per-shock parity
generally requires shorting, so its natural home is a long--short
mandate, not the long-only setting studied here.
(3) Richer universes. Cross-asset causal chains are plausibly
longer-lived than those among 99 co-moving large-caps, so
multi-asset-class portfolios are where the skeleton/orientation split
could differ materially.
(4) Orientation-aware risk attribution. The total-effect matrix $B$
supports ex-post decompositions that attribute a month's loss to the
shocks that drove it, which the correlation toolkit cannot express. This
is worthwhile even where the allocation gain is modest.
The infrastructure built here (the deterministic pipeline, the discovery
cache and the ablation harness) makes each of these a matter of days,
not months, of compute. As this project found twice, that is a
prerequisite for trusting the answers.

\section{The first phase: the driver route and its two null results}
\label{app:hsp}

The project's first phase built on Hierarchical Sensitivity Parity (HSP;
Section~\ref{sec:hrp-theme}). It asked whether replacing HSP's
correlation-based driver selection with selection from a discovered
causal graph improves the allocation. I evaluated three configurations
on the panel and protocol of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}. The first is
HSP as published: correlation-selected drivers, neural sensitivities,
and HRP on the sensitivity distance. The second is a causal-selection
variant that chooses the $K{=}17$ drivers by their aggregate outgoing
influence in the DYNOTEARS graph (also run under VARLiNGAM). The third
is a closed-loop variant that feeds each month's realised excess Sharpe
over equal weight back into the driver scores, through an exponentially
weighted utility with blend weight $\alpha$ and decay $\gamma$. Over the
252- and 504-day windows, a sweep over $K\in\{10,14,17,20,25\}$ and a
$3\times3$ grid over $(\alpha,\gamma)$, the phase contributes
\rsNtrialsPhaseI\ of the \rsNtrials\ trials in the deflation universe.
Two of them are the skeleton cells that the replication gate of
Section~\ref{sec:ablation-design} targets. The remaining
\rsNtrialsPhaseII\ are the graph-route cells of this study. Both of the
phase's results were null.

\paragraph{The claimed improvement is inside the method's own noise.} At
the 252-day window and $K{=}17$, causal selection beats HSP as published
by $+0.010$ net Sharpe ($p=0.27$). The sign reverses at $K=10$, $20$ and
$25$ ($-0.015$, $-0.016$, $-0.010$), and no difference at any $K$ is
significant ($p=0.10$--$0.77$). I re-ran the identical causal-selection
configuration under \rsSeedN\ network seeds, with discovery cached so
that the network initialisation is the only varying ingredient. Its net
Sharpe moves across $[\rsSeedMin, \rsSeedMax]$ (median $\rsSeedMedian$;
Figure~\ref{fig:seed}). The seed range ($\rsSeedRange$) is more than
twice the $+0.010$ edge, and the committed seed sits at the
$\rsSeedPct$th percentile of its own distribution, so at the least
favourable seed the edge all but vanishes. Plain correlation-distance
HRP (\HRP{}) beats HSP as published at the same window ($\rsCorrSharpe$
against $0.371$), so the driver-and-network machinery subtracts value
relative to textbook HRP. The $90\%$ Model Confidence Set over the
\rsMcsUniverse\ distinct 252-day strategies of
Section~\ref{sec:believe-rigour} retains \rsMcsSize\ of them and
eliminates only HSP as published. Every graph-route allocator of
Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity} is deterministic, and at the 252-day
window each sits above the seed distribution's maximum.

\paragraph{The monthly feedback loop cannot learn.} Across the full
$\alpha\in\{0.4,0.6,0.8\}\times\gamma\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5\}$ grid, the
closed loop's weights equal the open-loop variant's at every rebalance.
This is because the utility blend can only re-rank drivers within the
causally selected set. Even where it could act, its signal is too weak
to act on. The monthly reward is a Sharpe ratio estimated from 21 daily
observations, whose sampling standard error is about $\rsSharpeSE$.
Across 215 rebalances it has mean $\rsRewardMean$ and standard deviation
$\rsRewardStd$, a per-update signal-to-noise ratio of about
$\rsRewardSNR$. A feedback rule at this frequency receives one number a
month that is almost entirely noise, so no learning rule could extract
the signal without lengthening the reward window or raising the update
frequency.

\section{Hyperparameters}

The settings used, for reproducibility. Discovery: DYNOTEARS lag $p=1$,
$\lambda_W=\lambda_A=0.05$, fit-time edge threshold $0.01$ (the
extraction threshold $\tau$ of Section~\ref{sec:asset-graph} defaults to
$0$ on top of this); VARLiNGAM with lingam pruning disabled (adaptive
lasso fails at $d>n$); ridge-Granger ridge $\lambda=10^{-2}$ scaled by
window length, density-matched per window to the paired DYNOTEARS graph.
Allocators: single-linkage where clustering is used;
$\Sigma_{\mathrm{SEM}}$ with diagonal shock variances from fit-window
residuals, nearest-PSD projection and ridge load
$10^{-6}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)/N$; topological tie-break by total
downstream influence then name. Protocol: 5\,bps one-way (swept
$\{0,5,10,20\}$); windows 189/252/378/504 trading days; holding and step
21 trading days; 215 (189- and 252-day windows) / 209 (378-day) / 203
(504-day) rebalances, 2007-01--2024-11 (rebalances before a window's
burn-in completes fall back to equal weight, identically for every
allocator at that window); Politis--Romano bootstrap, 10{,}000
resamples, mean block 21 trading days.

\section{Reproducibility}

All results derive from committed code and cached data and are exactly
reproducible. After the data-determinism fix of Section~\ref{sec:cache},
two independent end-to-end runs produce identical graphs, weights and
NAVs. The replication gate verifies this: \skelsamp{} against the prior
phase's committed bundle gives $\Delta$Sharpe $= 0$ and a maximum weight
difference of $0.0$ over all 215 rebalances, and I re-verified it on the
4-window grid by an exact repeat of a 378-day cell. The ablation ran on
1{,}684 cache-verified graph fits with a 100\% hit rate, and each
backtest regenerates in ${\approx}40$ seconds from the cache.

\paragraph{Cache mechanics and the determinism defect.} The discovery
cache is keyed by the exact window bytes, the column set, the method and
the hyperparameters, with atomic writes. A key miss halts the run rather
than silently refitting a subtly different graph. The data-determinism
defect of Section~\ref{sec:cache} arose because EEM's 2003 inception
fell inside the padding window of the cache-coverage check. This
triggered a silent re-download whose auto-adjustment jittered values by
${\approx}3\times10^{-7}$ from run to run. The non-convex DYNOTEARS
optimiser amplified this into graph changes of ${\approx}0.14$ in
$\lVert W\rVert$. VARLiNGAM's ICA-on-residuals fits were essentially
unchanged by the same jitter, which is a small robustness result in its
own right.

\paragraph{Environment and data access.} I used Python~3.13 in the
repository's virtual environment. \texttt{causalnex} and \texttt{lingam}
are vendored (source-tree imports via \texttt{\_vendored.py}), not
pip-installed, so the repository itself pins their exact versions. All
commands run from the repository root, from which \texttt{pipeline}
imports as a package. Raw market data requires a WRDS/CRSP licence, but
the fitted graphs and derived series are committed to the content-keyed
cache, so every result in this report regenerates offline without it.

\paragraph{Regeneration order.} The report is reproduced in four steps,
each idempotent against the cache. (1) Run the unit-test suite (60+
tests, including the orientation-blindness and orientation-sensitivity
properties of Section~\ref{sec:ablation-design}). (2) Run the backtest
grid, which verifies every graph fit as a cache hit (a miss halts the
run rather than silently refitting). (3) Rebuild the statistics macros
with \texttt{scripts/robust\_stats.py -{}-phase-ii}. (4) Rebuild the
figures with \texttt{scripts/plot\_phase\_ii\_figures.py} and compile
the report.

\paragraph{Provenance.} Every statistic quoted in the text is either a
generated macro (\path{_generated/robust_stats.tex}) or traceable to a
committed CSV (\path{results/phase_ii_matrix.csv},
\path{phase_ii_contrasts.csv}, \path{robust_stats_phase_ii.csv},
\path{regime_analysis/}, \path{oos_slice.csv}/\path{oos_contrasts.csv}).
Every figure is regenerated by the plotting script above. No result in
the 2007--2024 panel depends on a live network fetch.

\paragraph{The out-of-sample slice.} The 2025--26 slice of
Section~\ref{sec:oos} is regenerated by
\texttt{scripts/run\_oos\_slice.py} (per allocator and window) followed
by \texttt{scripts/collate\_oos.py}. Its protocol, price-source rule and
outcome interpretations were committed in advance in
\texttt{PREDICTIONS\_OOS.md}. The slice keeps its own price and driver
caches (\path{cache/prices_oos}, \path{cache/drivers_oos}) so the
2007--2024 CRSP caches are never rewritten. Its asset prices come from
Yahoo Finance because CRSP daily data ends on 2024-12-31 (verified
against WRDS on 2026-08-15). Its DYNOTEARS fits join the shared
content-keyed discovery cache under their own keys.
