\section{Context and motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Portfolio optimisation divides capital across assets so as to maximise return
for a given level of risk. Since Markowitz's mean--variance model
\cite{markowitz1952portfolio}, almost all of its machinery has rested on one
object: the covariance --- equivalently, the correlation --- matrix of returns.
The most successful recent representative of this tradition, Hierarchical Risk
Parity (HRP) \cite{lopezdeprado2016hrp}, sidesteps the notorious instability of
inverting a noisy covariance matrix by \emph{clustering} assets on a
correlation-derived distance and allocating by recursive bisection down the
resulting tree. HRP and its descendants, including Hierarchical Sensitivity
Parity (HSP) \cite{rodriguez2023hsp}, therefore inherit correlation's
weaknesses at the very first step of the algorithm. Correlation is
\emph{undirected}: it records that two assets co-move, but not which one moves
the other. It is \emph{symmetric by construction}, so the clustering step of
every hierarchical allocator is structurally incapable of representing a
one-way relationship --- if asset $A$ drives asset $B$ but not conversely,
the correlation matrix, and hence the dendrogram, cannot say so. And it can be
\emph{spurious}: two assets responding to a common cause are statistically
indistinguishable from two assets genuinely linked \cite{simon1954spurious,
lopezdeprado2023causal}.

Causal discovery offers a richer input. Score-based structure learning ---
NOTEARS \cite{zheng2018notears} and its temporal extension DYNOTEARS
\cite{pamfil2020dynotears} --- makes it tractable to fit, at every monthly
rebalance, a directed acyclic graph (DAG) over a hundred assets: an estimate
not just of which assets are linked but of \emph{which way influence flows}
between them. A small literature now applies such graphs to equity markets
\cite{howard2025causal,tang2024trading}, but stops short of the allocation
step: no prior work sets hierarchical portfolio weights from the discovered
graph, and none asks what the graph's \emph{directionality} is worth once it
gets there.

That question has a sharp internal structure, because a DAG carries two
separable kinds of information. Its \emph{skeleton} --- the undirected pattern
of which asset pairs are causally linked --- can be handed to an unmodified
hierarchical allocator by symmetrising the adjacency matrix. Its \emph{edge
orientations} --- the direction of each link --- cannot: a symmetric
clustering step discards them entirely. Exploiting orientations therefore
requires new allocation machinery, and building and testing that machinery is
the methodological contribution of this dissertation. If the machinery adds
nothing, the interesting conclusion is that the skeleton was all that
mattered; if it adds value, hierarchical allocation has been leaving
information on the table.

\section{The scientific question}
\label{sec:question}

The dissertation answers one precisely-stated question, returned to
throughout:

\begin{quote}
\noindent\fbox{\parbox{\dimexpr\linewidth-2\fboxsep-2\fboxrule\relax}{%
\textbf{Q.}\ \emph{When the correlation matrix in hierarchical portfolio
allocation is replaced by a discovered causal graph, how much of the
performance gain is attributable to the graph's \textbf{skeleton} versus its
\textbf{edge orientations}?}}}
\end{quote}

\noindent Every term is operationalised and held fixed for the whole study:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item \emph{Hierarchical portfolio allocation}: HRP's two-stage scheme ---
    a symmetric distance matrix determines an asset ordering, and recursive
    bisection with inverse-cluster-variance splits determines the weights ---
    long-only, fully invested, on the ${\sim}100$ largest S\&P~500
    constituents (a fixed 99-name universe), rebalanced monthly over
    2007--2024 (215 rebalances).
  \item \emph{The correlation matrix}: the graph-blind control \textsc{corr}
    is textbook HRP --- distance $\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\rho_{ij})}$ on the
    lookback window's sample correlation \cite{lopezdeprado2016hrp}.
  \item \emph{A discovered causal graph}: the contemporaneous asset--asset
    block of a DYNOTEARS fit on the joint driver--asset panel at each
    rebalance (primary), with VARLiNGAM \cite{hyvarinen2010varlingam} and a
    ridge-regularised Granger-causal graph \cite{granger1969investigating} as
    comparators; one- and two-year estimation windows (252/504 trading days).
  \item \emph{Performance gain}: out-of-sample (walk-forward), net-of-cost
    (5\,bps per unit one-way turnover, swept over $\{0,5,10,20\}$\,bps),
    annualised Sharpe ratio of daily portfolio returns --- supplemented,
    because daily returns here have excess kurtosis
    ${\approx}\,\rsKurtosis$, by the Probabilistic and Deflated Sharpe ratios
    \cite{bailey2012psr,bailey2014deflated} and adjudicated family-wise by
    White's Reality Check, Hansen's SPA and the Model Confidence Set
    \cite{white2000reality,hansen2005spa,hansen2011mcs} over all \rsNtrials\
    configurations evaluated in the project.
  \item \emph{Skeleton versus orientations}: a \emph{fixed-graph ablation}.
    The identical per-window graph $M$ feeds allocators that differ only in
    what they read from it: D0 symmetrises $M$ before clustering (skeleton
    only), while five orientation-aware allocators (D1, D2, D2s, D3, D4;
    Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity}) consume the directed matrix itself. The
    decomposition is then two differences with a shared anchor:
    $\underbrace{\text{D0}-\textsc{corr}}_{\text{skeleton}}$ and
    $\underbrace{\text{D}^{*}-\text{D0}}_{\text{orientation}}$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Q presupposes two requirements, each tested in its own right:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{R\arabic*}, itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item \emph{The causal graph must improve on the correlation matrix at all}
    --- otherwise there is no gain to decompose
    (Section~\ref{sec:r1}).
  \item \emph{The allocator must be able to exploit directional information}
    --- symmetric clustering cannot, so the orientation-aware allocator
    family of Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity} is built, verified to respond to
    edge direction (and only the treatments to respond: the controls are
    provably orientation-blind), and pre-registered before the results were
    run (Section~\ref{sec:ablation-design}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Challenges, novel contributions, and quantified benefits}
\label{sec:challenges}

Following the recommended framing, the project is organised around three
technical challenges (C1--C3), the contributions addressing them (A1--A3),
and the benefits actually measured. Each maps onto Q: C1 supplies the graph,
C2 supplies the allocators that decompose it, C3 supplies the inference that
adjudicates it.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Key technical challenges.}
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*}]
    \item \emph{Discovering a credible asset--asset causal graph at every
      rebalance.} The graph must be estimated over a ${\sim}130$-variable
      joint panel of assets and macro drivers, under an economically-motivated
      directional prior (drivers may cause assets; single stocks do not cause
      the 10-year Treasury yield), 215 times per configuration, cheaply enough
      to sweep --- and \emph{reproducibly}: a graph that changes between runs
      invalidates every downstream comparison.
    \item \emph{Feeding a directed graph to an allocator that fundamentally
      consumes symmetric matrices.} Hierarchical clustering requires a
      symmetric distance; naive symmetrisation discards exactly the
      orientations in question. Exploiting them requires allocation machinery
      that has no analogue in the HRP/HSP literature --- and an experimental
      design in which orientation is the \emph{only} treatment variable.
    \item \emph{Attributing small performance differences honestly.} The
      candidate effects are $0.01$--$0.03$ Sharpe on heavy-tailed returns,
      produced by a study that evaluated \rsNtrials\ configurations; the
      framework this project began from adds two further hazards ---
      seed-dependent neural sensitivity estimates and a monthly feedback
      signal whose sampling noise dwarfs it.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Contributions addressing each challenge.}
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{A\arabic*}]
    \item A \emph{reproducible rolling causal-discovery pipeline}
      (Chapter~\ref{chap:discovery}): DYNOTEARS with the directional prior
      enforced natively and empirically verified to matter; a content-keyed
      discovery cache that makes the full ablation grid tractable
      (${\sim}700\times$ fan-out speedup); a data-determinism defect found and
      fixed, making every backtest bit-reproducible; and a single extraction
      chokepoint guaranteeing every allocator sees the byte-identical graph.
    \item The \emph{direction-aware allocator family and the fixed-graph
      ablation} (Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity}) --- the core methodological
      novelty: a structural (SEM-implied) covariance that propagates shocks
      through all directed paths of the DAG; \emph{causal-ordered bisection},
      which replaces the clustering dendrogram with the DAG's topological
      order; structural-shock risk parity; and co-ancestry clustering ---
      each deterministic, seed-free, and evaluated against matched
      skeleton-only and correlation controls on the identical graphs.
    \item A \emph{multiplicity-, distribution- and stability-aware evaluation}
      (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}): stationary-block-bootstrap contrasts
      for every pre-registered comparison; the PSR/DSR/Reality-Check/SPA/MCS
      battery over all \rsNtrials\ trials; a 20-seed audit quantifying the
      neural instability of the incumbent HSP route; and a quantified account
      of why its monthly feedback loop cannot learn (the measurement
      problem).
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Quantified benefits actually measured} (full detail in
    Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}; net of 5\,bps costs, 215 monthly
    rebalances, 2007--2024, Politis--Romano block bootstrap
    \cite{politis1994stationary}). Reported as measured, including where the
    answer is ``little'' or ``nothing''.
  \begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
    \item \textbf{The decomposition (the answer to Q).} At the one-year
      window, replacing the correlation matrix with the causal graph is worth
      up to $+0.031$ net Sharpe ($0.411$ vs $\rsCorrSharpe$), and the split is
      \emph{skeleton-dominated}: $+0.022$ from the skeleton ($p=0.14$),
      $+0.009$ from orientation ($p=0.47$). At the two-year window the split
      \emph{inverts}: $+0.009$ from the skeleton, $+0.023$--$0.027$ from
      orientation (pairwise $p=0.05$--$0.07$). No step is family-wise
      significant (SPA $p=\rsSpaSkelWtwo$--$\rsSpaDirWtwo$ range across
      tests and windows).
    \item \textbf{What orientation buys is robustness, not level.} The
      orientation-aware D1 ($0.411/0.395$) and D2s ($0.406/0.399$) are the
      only allocators strong at both windows; the skeleton-only allocator
      collapses from $0.403$ to $0.372$ at two years. The orientation edge is
      invariant to transaction costs up to 20\,bps and to the graph's
      sparsity threshold.
    \item \textbf{The incumbent driver route is dominated.} The HSP-style
      driver-selection pipeline (V1) is $K$-fragile, its monthly feedback
      loop is exactly inert across a $3{\times}3$ hyperparameter grid (its
      reward has signal-to-noise ${\approx}\,\rsRewardSNR$), and its neural
      sensitivity step makes its Sharpe seed-dependent
      ($\rsSeedMin$--$\rsSeedMax$ over \rsSeedN\ seeds --- a range exceeding
      its claimed $+0.010$ edge over its own baseline). Every deterministic
      graph-based allocator outperforms it under every seed.
    \item \textbf{Novelty relative to the closest prior work.} Howard et
      al.\ \cite{howard2025causal} build causal graphs on the S\&P~500 but
      never set weights from them; Rodr\'iguez-Dom\'inguez
      \cite{rodriguez2023hsp} allocates hierarchically but from correlation-
      selected driver sensitivities. Setting hierarchical weights directly
      from a causal graph, decomposing skeleton from orientation on fixed
      graphs, and the orientation-aware allocator family itself are, to my
      knowledge, new.
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

The honest one-line summary: \emph{the causal graph's value to hierarchical
allocation lies mostly in its skeleton at short estimation windows; its
orientations add a modest robustness effect at long windows; and neither
component's contribution survives family-wise data-snooping correction.}
Establishing this decomposition precisely, reproducibly, and with its
uncertainty stated, is the contribution.

\section{Report structure}
\label{sec:structure}

Chapter~\ref{chap:background} analyses the three works this study builds on
--- HRP/the backtest-rigour agenda, causal graphs in equity markets, and HSP
--- and locates the gap. Chapter~\ref{chap:discovery} (A1) presents the
discovery pipeline that produces the graphs. Chapter~\ref{chap:sensitivity}
(A2) develops the allocator family and the fixed-graph ablation that make Q
answerable. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} (A3) evaluates: the two
requirements, the decomposition itself, and how much of it survives rigorous
inference. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} concludes with limitations and
future work.
