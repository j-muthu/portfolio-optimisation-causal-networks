\section{Portfolio optimisation}
\label{sec:po}

Modern portfolio theory \cite{markowitz1952portfolio} chooses weights $w$ to
minimise portfolio variance $w^\top \Sigma w$ for a given expected return, where
$\Sigma$ is the return covariance matrix. The solution requires inverting
$\Sigma$, which is problematic in practice: sample covariance matrices for large
universes are noisy and near-singular, so the optimiser amplifies estimation
error and produces unstable weights that perform poorly out of sample. Factor
models address part of this by attributing returns to a few common factors, each
asset carrying a vector of factor sensitivities (betas); CAPM
\cite{sharpe1964capm} uses a single market factor, which Arbitrage Pricing Theory
\cite{ross1976apt} generalises to several. Under these models risk splits into a
systematic component, reduced by managing factor exposure, and an idiosyncratic
component, reduced by diversifying across assets. These two forms of
diversification usually trade off against one another, and reconciling them is a
recurring theme in the literature.

\section{Hierarchical Risk Parity}
\label{sec:hrp}

Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP) \cite{lopezdeprado2016hrp} avoids matrix inversion
entirely. It proceeds in three steps. First, it converts the correlation matrix
into a distance matrix and applies hierarchical (single-linkage) clustering to
obtain a dendrogram of the assets. Second, it quasi-diagonalises the covariance
matrix by reordering assets so that similar assets sit close together,
concentrating large covariances near the diagonal. Third, it allocates capital by
recursive bisection: starting from the top of the tree it splits the asset set
into two clusters, computes each cluster's variance using inverse-variance
weights, and allocates between the two in inverse proportion to their variances,
recursing down the tree. Because it never inverts the covariance matrix, HRP
avoids the ill-conditioning that destabilises mean--variance optimisation, and it
outperforms minimum-variance and inverse-variance portfolios out of sample
\cite{lopezdeprado2016hrp}. Two properties matter here: the clustering step needs
only a symmetric, non-negative distance matrix, and the allocation step needs
only cluster variances. The two stages are cleanly separable, which is what lets
the present work swap in a different distance matrix while leaving allocation
untouched.

\section{Hierarchical Sensitivity Parity}
\label{sec:hsp}

Hierarchical Sensitivity Parity (HSP) \cite{rodriguez2023hsp} extends HRP by
changing \emph{what} the assets are clustered on. Instead of the correlation of
returns, it clusters on the similarity of assets' sensitivities to a set of
common drivers. The motivation is the \emph{commonality principle}: the optimal
drivers of a portfolio are those most frequently and persistently selected across
its constituents, both in persistence over time and in probability of being
causal. Rodriguez Dominguez \cite{rodriguez2023hsp,rodriguez2025causal} argues,
via the Reichenbach common-cause principle \cite{hitchcock2020reichenbach}, that
if the drivers satisfy the commonality principle then a conformal map exists
between the unconditional return space (where angles between asset vectors
represent correlations) and the sensitivity space (whose coordinates are the
common drivers). A conformal map preserves angles, hence correlation and
clustering structure, so idiosyncratic and systematic diversification can be
achieved at once, escaping the trade-off of Section~\ref{sec:po}.

For each asset, HSP trains a feed-forward neural network mapping driver values to
the asset's return and extracts the sensitivity vector
$s_i = (\partial f_i/\partial d_1, \ldots, \partial f_i/\partial d_K)$ by
automatic differentiation, averaged over the fit window. The sensitivity distance
$D_{ij} = \lVert s_i - s_j \rVert_2$ defines the matrix on which assets are
clustered, with recursive bisection and sample-variance weights as in HRP. The
weakness this project targets is that HSP still selects its common drivers by
\emph{cumulative correlation} with the constituents --- the correlation-based
selection that the commonality principle's appeal to causality was meant to move
beyond. The author also flags regimes, non-stationarity and overfitting as open
issues \cite{rodriguez2023hsp}.

\section{Causal discovery}
\label{sec:cd}

Causal discovery learns directed structure from observational data
\cite{spirtes2000causation,kaddour2022causalml}. A structural causal model
represents a system as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) over variables, each a
function of its parents and a noise term; the DAG is a Bayesian network when the
distribution factorises according to the local Markov condition. Reconstructing
DAGs from data classically requires the \emph{faithfulness} assumption: that
statistical independence in the data implies structural ($d$-) separation in the
true graph. Constraint-based methods such as PC and FCI
\cite{spirtes2000causation} test conditional independencies but scale
exponentially in the number of nodes and lean on strong faithfulness, which is
fragile in finance: markets contain many mutually offsetting causal relationships
that can leave $d$-connected pairs with small marginal correlations, breaking the
assumption \cite{howard2025causal}.

Score-based methods avoid invoking faithfulness by searching for the graph that
best fits a regularised score, but the acyclicity constraint is combinatorial.
NOTEARS \cite{zheng2018notears} reformulated acyclicity as a smooth equality
constraint, $h(W) = \operatorname{tr}(e^{W \circ W}) - d = 0$, where $W$ is the
weighted adjacency, $\circ$ the Hadamard product and $d$ the number of nodes,
turning structure learning into continuous optimisation solvable with standard
solvers, and making score-based discovery tractable at scale. The LiNGAM family
\cite{shimizu2006lingam} takes a different route, exploiting the non-Gaussianity
of the noise to identify a unique causal ordering without prior structure --- well
suited to financial returns, which are heavy-tailed and non-Gaussian. This work
uses the NOTEARS and LiNGAM families \cite{kaddour2022causalml,silva_causalinf}.

\section{Temporal causal discovery}
\label{sec:temporal}

Asset returns are time series, so I use dynamic Bayesian networks, which add
lagged variables to the graph. Edges split into intra-slice edges $W$
(contemporaneous, at time $t$) and inter-slice edges $A_1,\ldots,A_P$ (lagged,
from $t-p$ to $t$). In econometric terms this is a structural vector
autoregression (SVAR),
\begin{equation}
  X = X W + \sum_{p=1}^{P} Y_p A_p + Z,
\end{equation}
where $X$ is the $n \times d$ panel, $Y_p$ its lags, and $Z$ noise; acyclicity
applies only to $W$, since lagged edges point forward in time.

\textbf{DYNOTEARS} \cite{pamfil2020dynotears} applies the NOTEARS smoothed
acyclicity constraint to this SVAR, minimising a penalised least-squares loss
$\tfrac{1}{2n}\lVert X - XW - \sum_p Y_p A_p\rVert_F^2 + \lambda_W\lVert W\rVert_1
+ \lambda_A\sum_p\lVert A_p\rVert_1$ subject to $h(W)=0$. It is the primary method
here: score-based (no faithfulness assumption), scalable, and able to forbid
specific edges, which is exactly what enforcing the directional prior of C1
requires. \textbf{VARLiNGAM} \cite{hyvarinen2010varlingam} estimates an SVAR by
taking the non-Gaussian residuals of a reduced-form autoregression, applying
independent component analysis, and recovering a DAG over the instantaneous
effects; its non-Gaussian assumption matches financial returns, and it is
retained as a robustness comparator. \textbf{NTS-NOTEARS}
\cite{sun2023ntsnotears} replaces the linear edge functions with 1D convolutional
networks, allowing non-linear causal relationships and, in principle, unifying
discovery and sensitivity extraction in one model; it is examined here as a
reduced-scope probe (Section~\ref{sec:nts}).

\section{Causal methods in quantitative finance}
\label{sec:cd-finance}

The closest work is Howard, Lohre and Mudde \cite{howard2025causal}, who apply
DYNOTEARS to the S\&P~500 and build three use-cases on the discovered networks:
peer groups for factor neutralisation, a low-centrality factor (long peripheral,
short central stocks), and a market-timing indicator from changes in network
density. Their conclusion is measured: causal networks \emph{complement} rather
than consistently beat correlation-based methods --- they sometimes raise
risk-adjusted returns but are computationally intensive and hard to interpret.
Two of their findings bear directly on this project. First, the inter-slice
(lagged) weights are almost always negligible for daily and monthly returns, so
the contemporaneous block dominates --- evidence for retaining VARLiNGAM, which
models lags and non-Gaussianity explicitly, as a check. Second, they never use
the causal graph to set portfolio weights: their graph is mapped to an embedding
and clustered into peer groups. This project, by contrast, converts causal
structure directly into allocation. Tang \cite{tang2024trading} designs trading
strategies from time-series causal-discovery output (including VARLiNGAM) on
Chinese and US markets, finding profitable signals but identifying computational
cost and scalability as binding constraints --- a caution this project
encountered directly (Section~\ref{sec:nts}, Section~\ref{sec:cache}).

\section{Research gap}
\label{sec:gap}

Table~\ref{tab:gap} positions this work. The proposed closed-loop Causal-HSP
addresses all three gaps: it selects drivers causally (C1), unlike HSP's
correlation selector \cite{rodriguez2023hsp}; it routes that directional
information into a symmetric allocator through the sensitivity space (C2), unlike
Howard et al.'s embedding clustering \cite{howard2025causal}; and it adds a closed
feedback loop in which realised performance affects subsequent selection (C3). It
should be said at the outset that, as the evaluation will show, filling these gaps
yields a genuine but \emph{modest and non-robust} improvement --- the scientific
value lies as much in characterising the limits of the approach as in the gains.
