\chapter{A3 and Evaluation: Closed-Loop Feedback and Results}
\label{chap:evaluation}
% ============================================================

This chapter presents the closed feedback loop (A3, addressing C3) and the full
empirical evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:loop} describes the loop;
Section~\ref{sec:method} the evaluation methodology; Sections~\ref{sec:headline}
to \ref{sec:regimes} report the results --- the headline comparison, the
causal-versus-correlation question, the sensitivity to the number of drivers, the
(non-)value of the closed loop, the asset-only result, and the
regime-conditional analysis. Section~\ref{sec:believe} then steps back and asks,
under good epistemic norms, how much of this we should actually believe.

\section{Closing the loop}
\label{sec:loop}

Achievement A3 extends HSP, which has no feedback. After the weights $w[t]$ are
held for the month, the realised reward is the excess Sharpe ratio relative to an
equal-weight portfolio over the same holding period,
\begin{equation}
  R[t] = \mathrm{Sharpe}\big(\text{portfolio over } [t, t{+}h]\big)
       - \mathrm{Sharpe}\big(\tfrac{1}{N} \text{ over } [t, t{+}h]\big).
\end{equation}
The excess form controls for market-wide regime effects, so drivers are not
punished for a crash that hit everything. Reward is attributed to drivers in
proportion to how strongly each shaped the portfolio,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{credit}_d[t] = R[t]\,\frac{\mathrm{influence}_d}{\sum_{d'} \mathrm{influence}_{d'}},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{influence}_d = \sum_i \big| w_i[t]\, s_{i,d}[t] \big|,
\end{equation}
and each selected driver's utility follows an exponential moving average, which the
next selection blends with causal evidence:
\begin{equation}
  U_d[t] = \gamma\, \mathrm{credit}_d[t] + (1-\gamma)\, U_d[t-1],
  \qquad
  \mathrm{score}_d[t] = \alpha\, z\big(\mathrm{causal}_d[t]\big) + (1-\alpha)\, z\big(U_d[t-1]\big).
\end{equation}
Here $\gamma$ sets the utility's memory and $\alpha$ weights causal evidence
against history ($\alpha=1$ recovers open-loop V1). Lookahead discipline is
enforced by keying utility on the holding-period-\emph{end} date and asserting a
21-day gap on lookup, with a deliberately-leaky ``canary'' variant used in testing
to confirm the guard fires. The full closed-loop method is V2.

\paragraph{The measurement problem.} Whether such a loop can work at all is,
before anything else, a question of measurement, and the \emph{update frequency}
is central to it. The reward $R[t]$ is a Sharpe ratio estimated over a single
$21$-day holding window, so its sampling error is large: for a near-zero true
Sharpe the standard error of a Sharpe estimated from $n$ observations is
approximately $\sqrt{1/n}\approx\rsSharpeSE$ at $n=21$. Empirically, across the
$215$ rebalances $R[t]$ has mean $\rsRewardMean$ and standard deviation
$\rsRewardStd$ --- a per-observation signal-to-noise ratio of just $\rsRewardSNR$
(indeed the mean reward is marginally negative). A monthly update is therefore
overwhelmingly noise: the per-rebalance signal is roughly an order of magnitude
below its own sampling variability. This is one half of why the loop cannot learn
(Section~\ref{sec:loop-eval}); the other half is structural. The frequency is not incidental: a higher-frequency reward would
shrink this error, which is why the measurement problem, not the feedback rule,
is the first thing future work should change.

\section{Evaluation methodology}
\label{sec:method}

All variants are evaluated on the same backtest: the approximate S\&P~100
universe, monthly rebalancing over 2007--2024 (215 rebalances), $K=17$ calibrated
once on a 2006 burn-in, at two lookback windows --- 252 trading days (one year)
and 504 (two years) --- as the primary robustness check. Transaction costs of
5\,bps per unit of one-way turnover are charged (with sweeps over
$\{0,5,10,20\}$\,bps); results are net of costs. The \emph{risk-adjusted return} of Q is
made precise here. The headline measure is the annualised Sharpe ratio, reported
alongside CAGR, maximum drawdown, volatility, turnover and concentration. But the
Sharpe ratio assumes normality, and daily returns on this universe have excess
kurtosis ${\approx}\,\rsKurtosis$ (Figure~\ref{fig:retdist}); I therefore also
report the Sortino and Calmar ratios and, for the rigour question Q5, the
distribution-aware Probabilistic and Deflated Sharpe ratios
\cite{bailey2012psr,bailey2014deflated}. The controlled scientific comparison
holds the \emph{allocator} fixed and varies only the driver-selection mechanism,
so the standard-HSP variant V0 is the baseline; the equal-weight ($1/N$) portfolio
is the absolute reference used in the regime analysis. Pairwise differences in
Sharpe are tested with a Politis--Romano stationary block bootstrap
\cite{politis1994stationary} (2000 resamples), which respects the serial
dependence of returns. Because the study evaluates \rsNtrials\ distinct
configurations, those pairwise tests are supplemented in
Section~\ref{sec:believe-rigour} by a data-snooping-aware battery (Deflated
Sharpe, White's Reality Check, Hansen's SPA, and the Model Confidence Set) that
corrects for the multiplicity. Regimes are defined by NBER recession dates,
top-versus-bottom VIX quintiles, and named historical stress windows.

The headline reading, stated plainly:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item At the one-year window, the causal variants beat the correlation baseline,
        and the best are V0$'$ (0.403) and VARLiNGAM-V1 (0.399), both significant.
        DYNOTEARS-V1's edge ($+0.010$) is in the right direction but not
        significant.
  \item At the two-year window the entire effect disappears: V0, V1 and V0$'$ all
        sit at $\approx 0.372$ and VARLiNGAM-V1 \emph{underperforms} the baseline.
  \item The closed loop adds nothing anywhere: V2 equals V1 to four decimal places.
  \item In absolute terms every variant has a low Sharpe ($\approx 0.37$--$0.40$)
        and an $\approx 51\%$ drawdown: this is a study of \emph{relative}
        driver-selection quality within a long-only equity allocator, not of
        absolute performance or drawdown control.
\end{itemize}

\section{Does causal selection beat correlation? (Q1)}
\label{sec:causal-vs-corr}

The answer is a qualified yes at one year and a clear no at two years. Two
mechanisms are worth noting. First, the cum-corr baseline is structurally
tautological: V0 selects essentially the same $\approx 10$--$14$ drivers every
rebalance --- mostly international-equity ETFs and the rates curve --- because
these trivially co-move with US equity, whereas the causal selector rotates
across $\approx 26$ drivers that track the macro regime (rates and credit before
COVID, crude and credit at the April-2020 crash, safe-haven assets mid-crisis,
volatility and rates in the recovery). This qualitative difference is robust and
is the cleanest vindication of the causal approach, \emph{independent} of the
modest Sharpe gap. Second, the two discovery methods disagree substantially ---
mean top-$K$ driver Jaccard of only $0.34$ between DYNOTEARS and VARLiNGAM --- yet
both beat V0 at one year: concordance of conclusion despite divergence of
selection.

The window-dependence is the central negative finding and admits a plausible
mechanism for VARLiNGAM. Its identifiability rests on non-Gaussian residuals; a
two-year window spans more regimes, so by the central limit theorem the residuals
trend Gaussian, ICA identification weakens, the causal order becomes noisier, and
selection degrades --- consistent with VARLiNGAM being strongest at one year and
falling below baseline at two. DYNOTEARS, score-based and distribution-free, lacks
this specific sensitivity but also lacks the one-year significance. The
interaction between an algorithm's assumptions and the window length is itself a
useful methodological observation, and a caution against quoting the strong
one-year VARLiNGAM number without its two-year companion.

\section{Sensitivity to the number of drivers $K$ (Q3)}
\label{sec:ksens}

$K$ is calibrated from the data ($K=17$ by Kneedle), but a robustness sweep over
$K\in\{10,14,17,20,25\}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ksens}) shows the causal-versus-
correlation comparison is \emph{not} robust to it. At one year the V1$-$V0 edge is
positive only near $K=17$; it turns negative at $K=10$, $20$ and $25$, driven
mostly by the cum-corr baseline's Sharpe bouncing erratically between $0.37$ and
$0.40$ while V1 stays near $0.38$. None of the per-$K$ differences is significant.
At two years V1's Sharpe is flat at $\approx 0.372$ for $K\ge 14$ because the
greedy selector exhausts the $\approx 13$ drivers that clear the Stage-A threshold.
The honest implication is that the one-year DYNOTEARS edge holds at the
\emph{data-chosen} $K$ but should not be presented as $K$-robust; this, together
with the zero FDR-significant drivers found in calibration
(Section~\ref{sec:selection}), tempers the strength of the driver-based claim.

\section{Is the closed loop useful? (Q4)}
\label{sec:loop-eval}

No. Sweeping the feedback strength over the full grid
$\alpha\in\{0.4,0.6,0.8\}\times\gamma\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5\}$ at the one-year window
gives a Sharpe of $0.381$ at \emph{every} setting, identical to open-loop V1 to
four decimals (Figure~\ref{fig:grid}). The feedback machinery genuinely runs (the
utility table is populated at all 215 rebalances), yet the per-rebalance weights
are identical to V1 throughout. The mechanism is now understood: the utility blend
can only \emph{re-rank drivers within} the causally-selected set --- it cannot
promote a driver the causal selector never shortlisted --- so when the causal
top-$K$ is stable, the selected set never changes and V2 collapses to V1 exactly.
This structural inertness compounds the measurement problem of
Section~\ref{sec:loop}: with a per-rebalance reward signal-to-noise ratio of only
$\rsRewardSNR$, even where the loop \emph{could} re-rank the shortlist a single
month's reward is too noisy to do so reliably --- both point the same way.
This is the strongest possible form of the negative result: the closed loop is not
merely unhelpful on average, it is \emph{inert} across the entire feedback grid.
C3's hypothesis --- that feedback improves robustness --- is rejected for this
design.

\section{Do exogenous drivers help at all? The asset-only result (Q2)}
\label{sec:v0prime-eval}

The most striking result is that removing the exogenous drivers \emph{improves}
performance at the short window. Asset-only Causal-HRP (V0$'$) reaches Sharpe
$0.403$ at one year, significantly above both the correlation baseline
($+0.032$, $p<0.001$) and the driver-based Causal-HSP V1 ($+0.022$, $p=0.01$),
with the smallest drawdown ($-50.5\%$). On this universe and window, the
asset--asset causal graph alone is a better basis for clustering than the entire
driver-selection-and-sensitivity apparatus. A plausible reading is that the
exogenous-driver path adds estimation noise (driver selection, then a neural
sensitivity fit) that the direct asset-graph route avoids, while still capturing
the cross-asset structure that matters for clustering. This is an uncomfortable
result for a project framed around \emph{driver} selection, and it is reported as
the headline it deserves to be. The necessary caveat is that V0$'$ is also
window-dependent: at two years it falls back to $\approx 0.372$, level with
everything else.

\section{What survives rigour? (Q5)}
\label{sec:believe-rigour}

The second half of Q is the decisive one: does any measured advantage survive
correction for the multiplicity of the \rsNtrials\ configurations tried and for
the non-normality of returns? The pairwise bootstrap of
Section~\ref{sec:headline} answers a single pre-specified comparison; it does not
discount the search that produced it. Two corrections are applied.

\paragraph{Non-normality (PSR) and selection bias (DSR).} Daily returns are
strongly non-normal (Figure~\ref{fig:retdist}, excess kurtosis
${\approx}\,\rsKurtosis$), which the plain Sharpe ignores. The Probabilistic
Sharpe Ratio --- the probability the true Sharpe exceeds zero given the sample's
skewness, kurtosis and length --- is nonetheless high for every variant
(V0 $\rsVzeroPSR$, V0$'$ $\rsVprimePSR$, V1-DYNOTEARS $\rsVoneDynoPSR$,
V1-VARLiNGAM $\rsVoneVarPSR$): the \emph{level} of risk-adjusted return is real,
not a fat-tail artefact. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio then sets the bar to the best
Sharpe expected from \rsNtrials\ trials under the null of no skill. It deflates
every variant (V0 $\rsVzeroDSR$, V0$'$ $\rsVprimeDSR$, V1-DYNOTEARS
$\rsVoneDynoDSR$, V1-VARLiNGAM $\rsVoneVarDSR$; Figure~\ref{fig:dsrmcs}) but the
leaders remain positive --- so the short-window asset-only and VARLiNGAM Sharpes
are not merely the luckiest of many tries.

\paragraph{Does the best strategy beat the baseline (RC/SPA)?} The question Q
actually poses is comparative: does the \emph{best causal variant} beat the
correlation baseline once the search is accounted for? Taking net return as the
performance measure (the variants' volatilities are near-identical at
${\approx}\,16.5\%$, so this ordering mirrors the Sharpe ordering), White's
Reality Check gives $p=\rsSpaRC$ and Hansen's SPA $p=\rsSpaConsistent$ for the
best causal variant against V0 at the one-year window. Even after correcting for
the \rsNtrials-way search, these family-wise tests \emph{reject} the null that
the best causal variant fails to beat the correlation baseline: at one year, the
strongest causal-structure variant out-returns V0 by more than data-snooping
alone can explain.

\paragraph{Which strategies are indistinguishable (MCS)?} The Model Confidence
Set returns the variants statistically indistinguishable from the best at the
$90\%$ level. It contains just \rsMcsSize\ variants (\rsMcsMembers); the
correlation baseline V0 \rsMcsVzeroIn\ among them. The decisive --- and most
disciplining --- detail is \emph{which} variants survive: the asset-only V0$'$
and VARLiNGAM-V1, but \emph{not} the driver-based DYNOTEARS-V1 (which the set
eliminates alongside V0). The confidence set of best strategies is therefore
exactly the two causal-\emph{structure} variants, excluding both the correlation
baseline and the project's headline causal-\emph{driver-selection} method.

\paragraph{Verdict on Q5.} The battery splits Q into two answers. The absolute
risk-adjusted returns survive correction for non-normality and multiplicity (all
DSR ${>}\,0.92$), and at the one-year window the best causal-structure variants
\emph{do} clear a data-snooping-aware comparison against the correlation baseline
($p=\rsSpaRC$; V0 \rsMcsVzeroIn\ in the $90\%$ MCS). But the specific claim Q
puts first --- that causal \emph{driver selection} (DYNOTEARS-V1) beats
correlation --- is \emph{not} supported: that variant is itself eliminated from
the confidence set, indistinguishable from the baseline. What survives the most
stringent test is the asset-only and non-Gaussian-ICA \emph{structure}, not the
driver-selection mechanism the project was built around --- and, as the
two-year-window results above showed, none of it survives the move to a
two-year window. The data-snooping battery thus refines rather than erases the
short-window findings of
Sections~\ref{sec:causal-vs-corr}--\ref{sec:v0prime-eval}.

\section{Regime-conditional analysis}
\label{sec:regimes}

Because the hypothesised advantage of causal methods is robustness around regime
changes, I condition performance on market regime (Figure~\ref{fig:regime},
full tables in Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). At one year, every causal variant
beats the correlation baseline in \emph{every} regime slice --- NBER recession and
expansion, and high- and low-volatility --- and V0$'$ is the largest edge
throughout, including the least-bad recession Sharpe ($-0.575$ versus V0's
$-0.638$) and the smallest recession drawdown. This is the cleanest support for
the core hypothesis: where causal structure helps, it helps most in stress. The
caveat, again, is the window: at two years the regime edges collapse with the
aggregate ones.

\section{How much should we believe this?}
\label{sec:believe}

Good practice demands that the results be read against their own threats to
validity, stated explicitly rather than buried.

\paragraph{Effect sizes are small and mostly not significant.} The headline
DYNOTEARS edge is $\approx 0.01$ Sharpe and not significant; only the asset-only
V0$'$ and the one-year VARLiNGAM results clear conventional significance, and only
at one window. Over $\approx 215$ monthly observations a $\Delta$Sharpe of
$0.01$--$0.03$ is near the detection floor, so the study is plausibly
\emph{underpowered} for effects of this size, and absence of significance is not
evidence of absence of a (small) effect.

\paragraph{Multiple comparisons.} The study reports many comparisons --- four
variants, two methods, two windows, five values of $K$, a nine-point feedback
grid. Testing this many configurations inflates the probability of finding
\emph{some} significant result by chance, so the significant cells (V0$'$ at one
year, $p<0.001$; VARLiNGAM at one year, $p=0.004$) should be read with a
multiple-testing discount in mind. They are reported as exploratory findings, not
as pre-registered confirmatory tests, and the consistent direction across regimes
(Section~\ref{sec:regimes}) is offered as corroborating, not confirmatory,
evidence.

\paragraph{In-window-significant is not robust.} Every positive result here is
single-window: nothing that is significant at one year survives at two years. The
two-window design was deliberately adopted to expose exactly this kind of
fragility, and it did.

\paragraph{A result that needed a reproducibility fix.} As recorded in
Section~\ref{sec:cache}, two earlier conclusions (a two-year causal edge; a
``significantly worse'' closed loop) were artefacts of one non-deterministic
driver and vanished once the pipeline was made reproducible. This is a concrete
reminder of how fragile small financial-backtest effects are, and why the
corrected numbers above should be treated as the only ones that count.

\paragraph{What the evidence does and does not support.} It supports: (i) that
correlation-based selection is tautological and causal selection is qualitatively
more regime-adaptive; (ii) that, on this universe at the one-year window,
asset-only causal structure modestly but significantly improves risk-adjusted
return over correlation-HSP. It does \emph{not} support: a robust, large, or
reliably significant advantage of causal \emph{driver} selection; any benefit from
the closed-loop extension; or any of these effects at the two-year window. That
asymmetry --- a genuine but narrow positive, surrounded by clearly characterised
negatives --- is the scientific result.
