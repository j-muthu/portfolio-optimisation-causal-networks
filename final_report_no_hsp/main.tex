\begin{abstract}
\noindent Hierarchical portfolio allocators typically use 
the return correlation matrix as an input to their allocation. 
However, this lacks any notion of causality, and is therefore
vulnerable to spurious correlation.
Causal discovery algorithms ameliorate this, producing
a directed graph (DAG) which can be decomposed into a skeleton
(which assets are linked) and edge orientations
(which assets causally affect others).
This thesis addresses the following question: when the
correlation matrix in hierarchical portfolio allocation is
replaced by a causal graph, is there a performance
improvement, and how much of any improvement is attributable
to the graph's skeleton versus its edge orientations?

I answer this question by providing an identical DAG to
two classes of allocators: one that only takes in the graph's
skeleton, and one that takes in the whole graph, including its
edge orientations. This decomposes the performance gain into
the two aspects of the DAG. I then compare these allocators
to HRP as a baseline. I estimate this decomposition over 
2007--2024, using 4 estimation windows 
(9 months to 2 years), on a 99-asset subset of the S\&P~500. 
I use DYNOTEARS and VARLiNGAM as causal discovery algorithms,
with ridge-Granger as a robustness check.

I find that replacing the correlation matrix with a causal graph
yields a positive but statistically insignificant performance
improvement. I run 40 comparisons to the correlational HRP baseline
(covering 5 causal allocators, 2 causal discovery algorithms, 
and 4 estimation windows), with performance gains ranging 
from $-0.025$ to $+0.036$, with 36 out of the 40 comparisons 
yielding a positive gain. Hansen's SPA test does not reject the
null hypothesis of no performance gain across the full set of
7 (the 5 mentioned above, plus 2 robustness checks) allocators
at any of the 4 estimation windows, nor when accounting for
the search across the windows. The relative contributions of the
skeleton and the edge orientations depend on the estimation window.
Around the 1-year window, the skeleton is responsible for most of 
the graph-based allocators' performance gain. Edge orientations
make a positive contribution from the 1-year window and longer, with
the largest contribution at the longest windows, but this is
sensitive to the causal discovery method: only DYNOTEARS yields
this positive contribution, VARLiNGAM does not.
To test whether the performance contribution of the skeleton-based
allocator is truly attributable to the skeleton of the causal DAG,
I also test whether there is a similar performance gain from a
graph-free skeleton from a partial correlation network; I find no
such gain at the 1-year window, indicating that the performance 
gain is truly from the causal DAG's skeleton. 
Similarly, I test whether the performance 
contribution from the allocators using the graph's edge orientations
is simply due to the fact that the graph removes the 
common market factor, as the graph treats underlying shocks as 
independent. I find this is indeed the case: providing a de-factored
covariance matrix as an input to the allocators reproduces the
performance gain from the edge orientations, indicating that
the gain was from de-factoring rather than from knowledge of
edge orientations.

I find that replacing the correlation matrix with a causal DAG
yields a positive but statistically insignificant increase 
in the Sharpe ratio. Around the 1-year estimation window,
the graph's skeleton accounts for most of the performance gain,
while the edge orientations contribute most at the extremes
(9 month and 2 year windows). However, the edge orientations'
contribution is due to the DAG's removal of the common
market factor, as providing a de-factored covariance matrix
as an allocator input reproduces the performance gain from the
edge orientations.
\end{abstract}
